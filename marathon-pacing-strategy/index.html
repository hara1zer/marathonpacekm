Pacing decisions

Marathon pacing strategy: from target to race decisions

A pacing plan connects a supported goal with checkpoints and decisions for the day. Fitness, preparation, fueling, conditions and execution all matter; pacing cannot compensate for every gap.

Start with the target’s assumptions

A predictor supplies a model scenario. Review the input race, normal training weeks, recovery and course before turning that number into a goal. The goal-selection guide works through those decisions. A completed pace session is useful evidence, but not a certificate of marathon readiness.

Opening kilometres: settle into the plan

Compare your reliable splits with the plan while allowing for crowds and terrain. Early comfort is normal and is not a reason on its own to accelerate. A deliberately slower opening needs to be included in the finish-time calculation. See a four-hour opening example.

Middle kilometres: keep reviewing the context

Track effort, course changes and fuel actually taken. A hill or headwind can explain a slower split; sustained difficulty on comparable terrain deserves a broader reassessment. Pace, heart rate and perceived effort each have limits. Use them together.

Late kilometres: recognise the remaining task

After 30 km, 12.195 km remain. At 30 km in 2:55, retaining a four-hour target requires about 5:20/km from there. If you are already fading, accepting a revised goal may be more sensible than chasing that requirement. Work through an adjustment.

Change the plan when the day changes

  • Heat: review exposure and symptoms, not just a fixed pace penalty.
  • Wind: account for sheltered and exposed sections.
  • Hills: allow for climbs and controlled descents.

Stop for concerning symptoms and seek assistance. A pace calculation cannot establish safe participation.

Make fueling practical

Plan intake using products and quantities you have practised. Count carbohydrate from both gels and drinks, check station locations and distinguish planned intake from what you actually consume. The fueling guide provides complete worked schedules, and the calculator generates time reminders.

What my own races add

My two Run Melbourne halves show a fade and a stronger finish. My Sydney Marathon review shows why a stronger half and completed workouts did not guarantee the marathon I wanted. I use these records as examples with limits, rather than evidence of one universal formula.

Prepare a concise reference

Choose the checkpoints you will use, mark difficult course sections and write a fallback action. Use split-strategy arithmetic and the pace band to make the plan readable. Practise the watch setup within ordinary training; no extra proof workout is required.

Common questions

Is pacing more important than fitness?

Both influence the result, along with preparation and conditions. The evidence here does not rank them for every runner.

Must I run a negative split?

No. It is one possible plan. A controlled race can still have a slower second half.

Will a slower start prevent a fade?

It may address an overly fast opening, but no starting strategy guarantees the later outcome.
