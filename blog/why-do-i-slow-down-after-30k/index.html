Why Do I Slow Down After 30K in a Marathon?

A slowdown after 30 km is a pattern to investigate. Compare your splits with the course, conditions, preparation and actual fueling. This guide helps you review those observations without assigning a cause from the finish time alone.

Start with the recorded pattern

A slowdown is an observation, not a diagnosis. Use the review table below to compare elapsed splits, terrain, actual intake and symptoms before choosing a training change.

Why the late stages can feel different

Thirty kilometres is a useful checkpoint because much of the race is complete and 12.195 km still remain. It is not a physiological switch. Slowing can begin earlier, later or not at all.

Fuel availability, effort, heat, muscle fatigue, terrain and health can interact. A split chart shows when pace changed, but cannot by itself identify the cause.

Possible contributors to late slowing

  • Target and pacing: the planned pace may not fit the day, or the opening may differ from the plan.
  • Preparation: duration, continuity and recovery can differ from what the race demands.
  • Nutrition: actual intake and tolerance may differ from the practised plan.
  • Conditions and terrain: later sections may be hotter, windier or hillier.
  • Health and symptoms: illness, injury or heat illness require more than a pacing adjustment.

These are possibilities to investigate, not a ranked diagnosis. Split data alone cannot identify which one caused an individual fade.

Review the pattern without diagnosing it

On a small screen, swipe the table to see all columns.

Observations to investigate after a fade
ObservationQuestions to ask
Pace slows on climbsHow much does the course explain? Did effort rise too?
Effort rises on comparable flat sectionsWhat changed in conditions, preparation or pacing?
Fuel intake differs from the planHow many grams were actually taken and tolerated?
Pain, dizziness or other symptomsWas medical assessment needed? Do not treat this as a pacing diagnosis.

Several explanations can fit the same pattern. An empty feeling does not prove glycogen depletion, and cramps do not by themselves prove a sodium deficit.

What to do during the race

If you are slowing but otherwise well, reduce effort to something you can sustain and reassess. Follow your practised fuel and fluid plan as tolerated; do not double up products to compensate automatically for a missed feed. The original finish target may need to change.

Stop for concerning symptoms and seek assistance. Confusion, collapse, chest pain or severe unusual breathlessness are not a normal part of a pacing strategy. Healthdirect Australia: heatstroke and heat illness explains why exertional heat illness can be an emergency.

What to review before the next race

Review the whole build before adding more work: consistency, representative long runs, recovery, recent illness and practical fueling. Choose a change that addresses an identified gap. More mileage or a harder fast-finish run is not automatically the answer.

My Sydney race review documents a late slowdown despite completed pace sessions. My two half marathons provide a contrasting example of a fade and a stronger finish. Neither story identifies one universal cause or cure.

Write a plan you can review

Before the next race, record a supported target, opening checkpoints, fuel quantities and likely difficult sections. Afterward, compare the plan with what happened. Choose a specific change from that review rather than promising a “30K-proof” race.

Use checkpoint arithmetic and complete fuel schedules to make the plan reviewable.

FAQ

Does the wall always begin at 30 km?

No. It is a useful late-race checkpoint, not a physiological switch.

Can my splits tell me why I faded?

They show the pattern, but do not isolate the cause. Review terrain, conditions, preparation, intake and symptoms.

Will more gels prevent every slowdown?

No. Fueling is one influence, and actual intake and tolerance matter. Follow an appropriate practised plan rather than adding products automatically.

A recorded example from my Sydney build

Sydney began feeling difficult just before 30 km, and I first walked around 31–32 km. My recorded kilometre pace eased from 5:51 at kilometre 27 to 6:50 at kilometre 33. I felt empty rather than sore, and taking more gels became unappealing around the same time. These observations help describe the progression without reducing it to one cause.
