Plan the 21.1 km

Half-marathon pacing: build your plan from start to finish

By Davin Pinto · Published 25 October 2026 · Research and worked examples

A half-marathon plan needs an opening pace you can settle into, checkpoints you can read without mental arithmetic and a decision for when the target stops fitting the day. “Start easy and finish strong” leaves too much to work out while running.

Start with a realistic finish target, calculate an even schedule, then decide whether a slightly slower opening is worth the faster pace it requires afterwards. The tables and worksheet below make that trade-off visible.

Choose the finish time before choosing the opening pace

Use a recent race, your completed training and the actual course to set the target. A personal best from a different build is background information. A recent 5K can show speed, but it does not by itself establish how you will manage the last third of 21.0975 km.

Look for agreement between several pieces of evidence: recent race performance, repeatable work near the proposed effort, long runs you completed without an unusual recovery cost, and the training you sustained between them. Where they disagree, choose a range and explain the uncertainty to yourself before race morning.

Write down three choices: the time you will initially pace towards, the conditions that would make you slow down, and the checkpoint at which you might consider a small increase. That last choice needs permission to keep the same pace. You do not owe the race an acceleration at 15 km.

The site’s pace calculator has a half-marathon distance option. Use it for straightforward finish-time conversion. If you are using this half as evidence for a later marathon, read what a half-marathon result can and cannot establish about marathon pace afterwards; do not add a marathon prediction to the decisions you need during this race.

Half-marathon pace chart: use cumulative time at course markers

A half marathon is 21.0975 km. The final 1.0975 km after the 20 km marker still needs a budget. At two-hour pace it takes about 6:15, so 1:53:45 at 20 km is on schedule.

Use official distance markers with your elapsed timer, without auto-pause. A marker might not align with your watch’s next kilometre beep. The clock above the road might be gun time. Keep those references distinct.

Worked two-hour example: the average is 5:41.3/km. Running the first 5 km five seconds/km slower gives an opening pace of 5:46.3 and reaches 5 km at 28:51. You then need about 5:39.7/km for the remainder. The cumulative checkpoints become 57:10 at 10 km, 1:25:29 at 15 km and 1:53:47 at 20 km.

The first 25 seconds are a planned delay. They do not need to disappear in the next kilometre. If the required later pace looks ambitious, reduce the offset or revise the finish target. An overly cautious opening followed by a desperate chase is still a poor match between goal and execution.

If all you want is even pacing, enter zero and use that schedule. A negative split is a possible outcome, not a separate prize worth making the second half unmanageable.

Give each checkpoint one job

Start to 5 km: settle without chasing

Find clear running space without repeated surges. Check that your breathing and movement feel appropriate for the opening of a race this long. If the first kilometre was slow because the road was crowded, record the delay rather than treating it as an instruction to sprint. Your first 5 km checkpoint tells you how much time actually needs explaining.

At 5 km: compare the plan with the effort

If you are close to schedule and settled, continue. If you are ahead and the pace is harder than intended, ease back now. If you are behind, identify whether the reason was a one-off delay, an uphill section or an effort that already feels excessive. Those situations call for different decisions; the stopwatch alone cannot choose.

At 10 km: protect the middle of the race

You still have more than 11 km to run. Check the next section of course, your practiced drink or fuel routine, and whether the pace remains sustainable. Avoid increasing speed simply because the first half went well. Use heart rate and perceived effort as context, rather than borrowing an untested percentage of maximum heart rate from someone else’s race.

At 15 km: decide, rather than automatically accelerate

There are 6.0975 km left. If conditions, breathing and movement support it, make a small change you can assess over the next kilometre. If you are already working at the effort you expected for this point, holding pace is a successful decision. If the target has become unrealistic, choose a manageable effort and use the next marker to reassess.

At 20 km: remember the remaining distance

Use the finish line and course markings, not the moment your watch reaches 21.1, as the end of the race. A finishing push depends on the runner and the situation; it is not included as a guaranteed time-saving device in this plan.

My two Run Melbourne races: the opening split was only part of the story

In my recorded Run Melbourne comparison, the first five full watch kilometres averaged 5:33.4/km in 2025 and 5:15.4/km in 2026. The last five full kilometres, 16–20, averaged 6:24.2 and 5:08.6 respectively.

The 2026 opening was faster in absolute terms, yet I maintained a much steadier middle and finished those recorded kilometres quicker. “Start slower” cannot explain that comparison on its own. The more useful question is whether the opening fitted the fitness and preparation I brought to that race.

These are watch-lap groups, not official 5 km timing splits. The activities recorded different distances, 20.79 km and 21.27 km, and different activity times. I therefore do not use them to manufacture official race checkpoints or claim a controlled pacing experiment. Training, conditions and other factors were not held constant.

For this plan, the practical lesson is to set the target from current evidence, then judge the opening against that target. Copying somebody’s first five kilometres, including mine, misses the context that made them appropriate or inappropriate.

What if the schedule stops fitting the course?

Hills: allow pace to change when holding the number would require a large jump in effort. An even-speed worksheet has no elevation model. Read the hill-pacing guide and mark the course sections where you expect the flat schedule to be least useful.

Heat or headwind: reassess the target against actual exposure. Do not treat a calculator adjustment as a guarantee that a revised pace is safe or sustainable. Use the conditions guidance alongside the event’s instructions and how you feel.

Water stops or a missed split: a 20-second delay spread over 10 remaining kilometres is two seconds/km of arithmetic. It is not a demand to recover it. If you join a group after a delay, check the start-time offset before assuming their finish target is now yours.

A planned run-walk approach: the tables give average moving speed with no stops. Your run portions will need a different pace. Rehearse the ratio and transitions; do not compare the running pace alone with a continuous runner’s average.

What the research supports, and what it does not

Nikolaidis, Čuk and Knechtle’s 2019 analysis of the Ljubljana races found that half-marathon runners tended to slow across successive segments. It describes what occurred in one event; it does not prove that a particular opening offset is optimal. The five-second example here is our transparent arithmetic choice, not a result from that study.
