Why marathon pace feels harder some days: a decision guide

By Davin Pinto · Updated 4 November 2026 · Editorial policy

When the same pace requires more effort, first decide whether today's session is appropriate. Then review conditions, recovery, recent workload and the quality of the observations. One difficult run cannot establish lost fitness, but you do not need a repeated pattern before responding to symptoms or reducing an unusually demanding session.

On this page

  • Today's decision
  • What might have changed?
  • Useful observations to record
  • Change the whole workload
  • Review a repeated pattern
  • Questions and sources

What does harder-than-usual pace tell me?

It tells you the cost of the run feels different. It does not identify a cause. The older label “fatigue drift” on this site described this observation; it is not a diagnosis or a measured physiological threshold. Heart rate rising during a sustained run is a separate observation, covered in the cardiac-drift guide.

If the session is becoming much harder than intended, reduce the demand and reassess. Shortening or stopping may be more appropriate than trying to preserve the planned distance. Chest pain, fainting, confusion or severe unusual breathlessness require stopping and urgent help. A watch score or a reassuring average heart rate cannot rule out a problem.

What might have changed since the comparable run?

Start with observable differences. A familiar pace can coincide with a different route, warmer conditions, less sleep, a longer run or a demanding preceding session. More than one change can be present, and a comparison of two runs cannot isolate its effect.

  • Weather and exposure: compare actual conditions and the time spent in sun or wind. Use the heat guidance and wind guide for those specific questions.
  • Terrain and measurement: hills and surfaces change the task; GPS and heart-rate sensors add measurement limits. A short pace spike can be a measurement issue without explaining the entire run.
  • Recent workload: include long runs, workouts, gym sessions and the running between them. A session called “easy” can still add duration or leave you unusually tired.
  • Recovery and life demands: record sleep, work, travel, stress and how you feel rather than reducing recovery to one device number.
  • Food and fluid routine: compare what you actually ate and drank with your usual, practised routine. An empty feeling alone cannot identify glycogen depletion or a specific fluid deficit.
  • Symptoms or illness: these may call for assessment rather than another training test.

Which observations are useful enough to keep?

In Halson's review of training-load monitoring, interpreting fatigue involves multiple kinds of information. For a practical record, keep perceived effort and circumstances beside the device measurements. No single measurement establishes whether you should continue.

Do not add a demanding heart-rate-recovery test to a run stopped because you feel unwell. A post-exercise number cannot establish the cause of today's difficulty. For an ordinary easy-running decision, use the easy-run pace guide.

Choose a response before analysing the trend

Different observations need different responses
SituationResponse to consider
Concerning symptomsStop and seek appropriate help; do not wait for more sessions.
Effort is unusually hard without an obvious explanationReduce or stop the session and review the wider context.
A brief pace change explained by terrain or signal lossUse effort and subsequent reliable observations rather than chasing the number.
Repeated difficulty in comparable circumstancesReview the target, workload and recovery with your plan or coach; seek assessment when unexplained or persistent.

Healthdirect's heart-palpitations advice describes symptoms that warrant medical attention. This page is a training-decision aid, not an assessment of the cause of an abnormal reading.

How can I change the workout without creating catch-up work?

Change the part that no longer fits: the pace, the amount of work, the total duration or whether you run at all. For a hypothetical session of three 10-minute marathon-pace blocks, removing the final block is a different change from slowing all three. Record which choice you made so the training log describes the actual demand.

A slower pace does not automatically make the full planned duration appropriate. Equally, a cancelled block does not need to reappear tomorrow. Review the next workout and long run together before moving demanding sessions closer to each other. The interrupted-plan guide covers rebuilding the calendar.

Keep pace labels separate from measured intensity. A pace called “marathon pace” can feel much harder in a different context; that does not prove it has become your physiological threshold. The terminology guide explains the distinction.

When does a pattern deserve a broader review?

Compare runs with enough shared context to make the comparison useful. A repeated change in pace, effort, recovery and symptoms can justify reviewing the plan, but there is no universal number of runs that turns “noise” into a diagnosis. Do not require three hard sessions or a fixed waiting period before acting.

For a record of progress over time, use the Zone 2 progress guide. For deciding how much evidence a noisy comparison provides, use signal versus noise. Those are longer-term interpretation tasks; this page starts with today's response.

What if this happens on race day?

Reassess effort, symptoms, conditions and the remaining task. The original target is a plan, rather than an obligation. If you are otherwise well but the intended effort is not sustainable, use the mid-race adjustment guide to review the remaining distance without chasing lost time.

Do not treat an unusually difficult opening as proof of adrenaline, or a late fade as proof of one nutritional cause. Your fuel schedule and late-race review answer specific parts of that wider picture.

Questions about a harder-than-usual pace

Should one difficult workout change my marathon goal?

One session cannot settle the whole goal decision. Review its circumstances and your wider preparation. A symptom or a demand that is inappropriate today can still require an immediate change.

Must I finish three difficult sessions before changing the plan?

No. There is no three-session rule on this page. Repeated comparable observations help with interpretation; they are not permission to ignore a problem the first time.

Can HRV decide whether I should run?

A single HRV reading cannot make that decision. Consider symptoms, your measurement routine, recovery and the broader pattern.

How long will recovery take?

That depends on the cause. This guide cannot distinguish ordinary training fatigue, illness or injury from a few observations.

Sources and scope

  • Halson (2014): Monitoring Training Load to Understand Fatigue in Athletes. Context for combining load, recovery and performance observations.
  • Healthdirect Australia: heart palpitations. Assessment and symptom guidance.
