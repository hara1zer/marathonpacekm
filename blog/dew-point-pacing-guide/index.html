Dew point and marathon pacing: read the whole forecast

Dew point helps describe moisture in the air, but it cannot supply a universal marathon pace penalty. Combine it with air temperature, sun, wind, exposure and your own preparation.

By Davin Pinto · Updated 1 October 2026 · Editorial policy · Corrections

Dew point versus relative humidity

Dew point is the temperature to which air would need to cool to become saturated, given its moisture content. Relative humidity depends on both moisture and temperature. That is why a cool morning can have high relative humidity without the same heat load as a warm, muggy afternoon.

Dew point should not exceed air temperature under ordinary weather reporting conditions. If a forecast appears to show 14°C air temperature with a 20°C dew point, check that the readings use the same time, location and units.

Neither row defines a safe pace or guarantees safe participation. Dew point alone is not a heat-stress index and does not account for solar radiation or your acclimatisation.

When a fixed pace stops being useful

If maintaining the planned pace requires unexpectedly high effort, ease and reassess rather than waiting for a dew-point cutoff. The original goal may no longer be appropriate.

Heart rate can add context, but neither a heart-rate cap nor a feeling of early comfort proves that heat stress is controlled. Event warnings and medical advice take priority over a calculator.

Plan for the hours you will be outside

  • Check the hourly forecast across your expected race duration, not just at the gun.
  • Identify exposed sections and the actual locations of water and cooling.
  • Use a familiar fuel and fluid plan, adjusted with appropriate guidance for the conditions.
  • Decide in advance that changing the target or stopping is acceptable if the conditions or symptoms require it.

For a numerical planning illustration, use the pace adjuster and read its assumptions. Its percentage adjustment is not a personalised safety model.

Prepare without manufacturing heat stress

Use familiar sessions to understand how conditions affect your effort. You do not need to add an unusually long run in heat to prove tolerance. Gradual acclimatisation can help, but the appropriate exposure depends on health, preparation and conditions.

Choose cooler hours or reduce the workload when necessary. A poorly recovered hot session may tell you more about that combination of stressors than about lost fitness.

Signs and assumptions to avoid

Do not assume heavy sweating means you are safe, or that lack of thirst means no heat concern. A runner with heatstroke can still be sweating. Weakness, dizziness, headache or nausea in heat warrant stopping and assessment; confusion, collapse or poor coordination require urgent help.

Do not prescribe extra sodium from dew point or cramps alone. Avoid forcing large fluid volumes to satisfy a fixed schedule. Healthdirect Australia: heatstroke and heat illness provides heat-illness guidance.

Questions about dew point

Is there a dew point where everyone should slow by the same amount?

No. Temperature, radiation, wind, exposure, acclimatisation and individual response also matter.

Can high humidity matter on a cool day?

Yes, but relative humidity alone can exaggerate how similar two days are. Read temperature and dew point together.

Does a favourable forecast mean my original pace is safe?

No forecast or pace calculator can certify that. Preparation, health and your response during the race still matter.
