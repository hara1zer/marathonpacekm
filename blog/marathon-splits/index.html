Marathon splits explained: use checkpoints to make decisions

A split tells you where the race stands. Combine it with effort and conditions to decide whether to continue, settle or revise your target.

Cumulative time and segment time

A cumulative split is the total time at a checkpoint. A segment split is the time since the previous checkpoint. Keep the distinction clear when reading a pace band: the time printed beside 10 km usually means the total to 10 km, not the time for kilometres 5–10.

First 5 km: compare the opening with your plan

If you planned an even four-hour race and reach 5 km in 26:40, you are about 1:46 ahead of that reference. Feeling good does not make the faster target sustainable. Settle towards the planned effort without abrupt braking or weaving. A slow crowded start also needs context; do not immediately chase the lost seconds.

Use the opening-10K guide for a planned start and overpacing checks for an unexpected one.

By 10 km: look at the trend

One slow uphill kilometre says little about the whole plan. Several fast checkpoints on comparable terrain, accompanied by more effort than rehearsed, are a reason to reconsider. Feeling comfortable is compatible with appropriate pacing; it is not itself a warning sign.

Halfway: review both time and the remaining task

Ask whether your effort still fits the remaining distance, whether you can follow your practised fueling plan, and whether the course or weather is changing. Uncertainty does not prove you chose the wrong pace, but it is a reason to avoid an unplanned acceleration.

At 30 km: calculate the cost of keeping the target

Reaching 30 km in 2:55 leaves 65 minutes for 12.195 km to finish in four hours: about 5:19.8/km. That is considerably faster than the original 5:41.3/km average. If you are already fading, recognising this requirement can make revising the goal easier than chasing the original band.

At 40 km: remember the remaining 2.195 km

The last section is longer than two kilometres. At the exact four-hour average, it takes about 12:29. If you have capacity to increase effort, do so gradually; if you need to ease, use the remaining distance to update expectations.

Read the pattern without judging the runner

A slower second half can reflect terrain, weather, target choice, fatigue or several factors together. A faster second half can be deliberate or incidental. Compare negative-split planning with the recorded late-race patterns in my two half marathons.

Carry the checkpoints you will actually use

Choose a target-specific table such as 3:30, 4:00 or 4:30, or create a printable marathon or half-marathon pace band. A few meaningful checkpoints can be easier to use than reacting to every GPS change.

Checkpoint questions

Should all marathon splits be even?

Even pace is a useful reference on a stable course in suitable conditions. Hills, wind, heat and planned stops can make varied splits sensible.

Which checkpoint matters most?

Each answers a different question: early checks catch departures from the plan, halfway reviews the remaining task, and later checks help revise expectations. No checkpoint predicts the finish on its own.

Can a fast finish prove I should have started faster?

No. It is one piece of evidence to review alongside the whole race and your goal. My half-marathon comparison separates a strong finish from an exact negative split.
