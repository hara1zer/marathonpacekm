Track-session arithmetic

Yasso 800s: calculate the workout and question the prediction

By Davin Pinto · Published 25 October 2026 · Research and worked examples

The attraction of Yasso 800s is the neat conversion: 3:30 for an 800 m repetition becomes a 3-hour-30-minute marathon label. The problem is that a marathon contains much more running and no recovery jog every two laps. Treat the session as a workout you can describe and repeat, rather than a finish time you have earned.

This guide separates the familiar rule of thumb from the session’s actual workload. Calculate the rep pace, recoveries and total time first. Then decide whether that session belongs in your training.

What are Yasso 800s?

The familiar version builds towards ten repetitions of 800 m. The time in minutes and seconds is compared with a marathon time expressed in hours and minutes: a 3:30 repetition corresponds to a 3:30 marathon in the rule of thumb. Amby Burfoot’s account on Bart Yasso’s website describes the workout’s origin in Yasso’s own experience. That origin is an anecdotal observation, rather than a validation study across different runners.

Use equal-duration easy recoveries between repetitions. Runner’s World’s workout instructions describe that convention. Write down the recovery you actually use, because other versions differ.

An 800 m effort is two laps of a standard 400 m track in the measured lane. It is 0.8 km, slightly less than half a mile: half a mile is 804.672 m. If the venue has a different lap length, count the measured distance rather than assuming two circuits equal 800 m. The Melbourne tracks and paths directory distinguishes tracks from loops with other distances.

The name does not establish the right intensity for you. Do not take a desired marathon time, turn its digits into a repetition target and assume that effort must be suitable. A coach or an existing training plan should determine whether 800 m intervals fit the runner and the week.

Calculate the complete Yasso 800 session

Choose the number of repetitions, repetition time and recovery convention. Warm-up and cooldown are time budgets. The result includes recoveries only between repetitions: ten efforts have nine gaps. Put any final easy jog in the cooldown.

Recovery distance and total session distance are unknown without easy-running speeds. Drills, strides, extra standing time and travel are not included.

Worked example: ten repetitions at 3:30 make 35 minutes of faster running. Nine recoveries of 3:30 add 31:30, so the main set lasts 1:06:30. Add a 15-minute warm-up and 10-minute cooldown and the session is 1:31:30, before any drills or extra pauses. The fast distance is 8 km; it is not the distance of the whole workout.

At the same repetition time, six efforts with five equal recoveries make a 38:30 main set and a 1:03:30 session with those warm-up and cooldown budgets. That may be a different training decision even though the watch shows the same 3:30 splits.

The full-session column can also expose a scheduling problem. Ten five-minute efforts with equal recoveries take 95 minutes for the main set alone. Add the same easy-running budgets and the appointment is two hours. That is worth noticing before putting it beside a long run in the calendar.

Compare marathon-specific workout formats

Why a 400 m recovery is not automatically the same workout

A distance-based recovery takes as long as your recovery pace requires. A time-based recovery lasts the specified interval regardless of distance covered. Record both when possible. Otherwise “ten 800s in 3:30” leaves out a large part of the session.

Coach Raj Hathiramani’s account, for example, describes 400 m recoveries. That is a real variation in published workout descriptions. It should not silently be substituted for equal-time recovery when comparing results.

Here is an arithmetic example: jogging 400 m at 7:00/km takes 2:48. Across nine gaps, that is 25:12 of recovery. With ten 3:30 efforts, the main set lasts 1:00:12. Equal 3:30 recoveries instead make it 1:06:30. Neither total says which version is appropriate for you, but they clearly describe different rest periods.

Walking, jogging and standing for the same duration are also different session descriptions. If you change the recovery, surface, shoes or conditions, note it before treating a quicker set as a clean improvement in fitness.

Can Yasso 800s predict your marathon time?

The digit conversion gives a memorable label. It does not measure the endurance, pacing, fueling or conditions needed to run that time over 42.195 km. Two runners can complete the same intervals with the same recovery while having very different training histories and marathon outcomes.

A 2019 systematic review of marathon prediction equations found that no single equation was accurate across all runner abilities and experience levels. That review concerns prediction models broadly; it does not validate this particular workout or supply an error range for it.

For that reason, this calculator does not turn a completed set into a recommended marathon goal. It can show the traditional label for ten equal-recovery efforts, clearly marked as a rule of thumb. It supplies no promised accuracy such as “within five minutes” or “within fifteen minutes”. Those margins would require evidence for the specific protocol and population.

Use the race-based predictor alongside the marathon-pace readiness guide to compare recent races, sustained training and marathon-specific execution. Those tools also have limits. Agreement between several observations is more useful than making one attractive number overrule everything else.

Failing to hold the planned repetition pace is not proof that your entire build has failed either. Check the session conditions and fatigue first, and discuss the pattern with your coach if you have one. A training log is more informative than a pass/fail badge.

Plan the session you can explain afterwards

If intervals are new to your training, do not begin with the ten-repetition version merely to make the rule work. Establish the purpose, appropriate effort, repetition count and recovery with the plan you are following. This article’s shorter examples demonstrate workload arithmetic, rather than prescribe a progression.

Use measured marks when available. Run the first effort with enough control to reproduce it, then record the actual time of each repetition and recovery. A fast final rep does not erase a series that became steadily slower. Stop the session if pain changes your stride or you cannot maintain controlled movement.

Book enough time for the whole visit. If you use a treadmill, consult the speed-to-pace chart and allow for belt acceleration; the constant-speed arithmetic here does not account for it. If you are comparing this with threshold work, the tempo, threshold and marathon-pace guide explains why changing the label does not establish an individual intensity.

Copy into your training log

Date and surface: __________ · Session purpose: __________

Reps planned / completed: __________ · Rep times: ____________________

Recovery type, time and distance: _________________________________

Warm-up / cooldown / actual total time: ___________________________

Effort, conditions and how the following easy run felt: ______________

Repeating that description later gives you something meaningful to compare. Calling the set a marathon prediction adds certainty that the session itself cannot provide.
