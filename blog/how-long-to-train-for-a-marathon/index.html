How long does it take to train for a marathon?

By Davin Pinto · Published 25 October 2026 · Research and worked examples

A 16-week plan starts sixteen weeks before the race. Your preparation may need to start much earlier. The useful question is whether you can repeat the opening weeks of that plan with the running you already do.

Many marathon programmes run for 16–20 weeks. A 12-week block can also appear on a calendar, but shortening the calendar leaves less room to establish the running base, practise longer outings and respond to interruptions. Use this guide to compare the actual workload and work backwards from race day.

Plan the lead-in
  • 12, 16 or 20 weeks?
  • Calculate your start dates
  • Compare your baseline with week one
  • Three runners, different starting points
  • Budget the time around the runs

How many weeks does marathon training take?

Allow a marathon-specific block of roughly 16–20 weeks, plus any preparation needed before week one. This is a planning range seen in published programmes, rather than a biological deadline. The London Marathon offers 16-week plans; the B.A.A. publishes 20-week plans. Their entry workloads and target runners matter as much as their length.

These are editorial comparisons, not eligibility rules. No number of weeks guarantees a finish time or freedom from injury. A runner starting from little or no current running may need a substantial period before any of these blocks.

Read week one before you choose the plan

A label such as “beginner” usually describes experience at the marathon distance. It need not mean beginner at running. The B.A.A.'s Level One description, for example, starts at about 25 miles per week—roughly 40 km. Someone currently running 12 km a week would have a very different starting point, despite both runners preparing for a first marathon.

Write down your last four complete weeks, including any zero weeks. Add your usual running frequency, recent longest outings, how you recovered and the time available. Then place those beside the first two or three weeks of the proposed programme.

  • Distance: are the opening weeks close to a routine you have actually sustained?
  • Frequency: does the plan add running days as well as kilometres?
  • Intensity: does it introduce workouts you have not been doing?
  • Long runs: are the opening long runs familiar, and how much of the whole week do they occupy?
  • Recovery: can you get through the ordinary week without persistent symptoms or mounting fatigue?

Changing all five at once makes it hard to judge what you tolerate. If the opening workload is a large jump, choose a different starting structure or allow more preparation. A neat percentage increase does not make an unsuitable starting point safe.

The four-week training planner accepts recent running and available days. It can help organise the next month. Its output still needs review; it is not a test that clears you to enter a marathon block.

Three runners can need three different lead-ins

Runner A: already training consistently

This hypothetical runner has repeated several months around 45 km per week over five days and is recovering well from regular long runs. A programme starting with a comparable workload is a more plausible fit than one that doubles the distance or adds unfamiliar hard sessions. They might compare a shorter specific block with a longer one by looking at the session sequence, rather than assuming more weeks must be better.

Runner B: a recent half-marathon finish, interrupted training

A completed half marathon is useful race evidence. It does not describe the weeks since. If this runner has had illness, missed weeks or new pain, the old result cannot substitute for a current baseline. They first need an appropriate routine they can repeat; see revising a disrupted training plan. Compressing the missed work into twelve weeks would not resolve that gap.

Runner C: starting to run

A runner doing occasional short outings has both a running routine and marathon preparation to develop. Their calendar should allow for those separate tasks. A later event or a shorter-distance goal may give them a more workable starting point. A universal “couch to marathon in X weeks” promise cannot assess the factors that would matter here.

These runners are invented examples. For a real account of preparation that did not produce the hoped-for marathon result, read Davin's Sydney review. The rebuild article describes intentions for a subsequent cycle, rather than a completed success story.

Budget hours, not just kilometres

A hypothetical 40 km week averaging 6:30/km contains 4 hours 20 minutes of running. Four 15-minute allowances for changing, travel or setup add another hour. Two 30-minute strength sessions bring that example to 6 hours 20 minutes, before any additional recovery time or unusually long journeys.

That is why a plan can fit your fitness and still fail to fit your week. Put the longest outing on the calendar first, then the remaining runs. Check whether the weeks around work trips, school holidays and family commitments are realistic. The two-session strength guide shows how to place gym work beside different running frequencies.

Keep the taper inside the block you choose. A sixteen-week programme with a taper does not mean sixteen full training weeks followed by extra taper weeks.

Choose the next month before the distant peak week

Compare programmes using their opening workload, where their harder weeks fall and what happens when you need to repeat or reduce a week. Save one working plan and review what you actually complete. An aspirational peak week is less useful than a starting month you can sustain.

Once the routine is established, use the long-run comparison to understand different session demands. Choose a finish-time target using current race and training evidence, rather than choosing the target solely because a plan is named after it.

Questions about marathon preparation time

Is sixteen weeks enough for a first marathon?

It can be a suitable block length for a runner who already meets that programme's starting demands. It cannot establish readiness for someone beginning from a very different workload.

Can I train for a marathon in three months?

Twelve or thirteen weeks leaves limited room to build a missing base. Compare the actual opening workload and your recent routine, then consider a later race if the gap is large. Avoid cramming weeks together.

Does a twenty-week plan mean slower progress?

No. The extra weeks may contain preparation, recovery or a different distribution of sessions. Count what is prescribed across the whole block before comparing plans by length.
