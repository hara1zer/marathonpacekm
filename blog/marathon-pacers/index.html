Running with a pace group

Marathon pacers: should you follow a pace group?

By Davin Pinto · Published 25 October 2026 · Research and worked examples

A marathon pacer makes the next kilometre easier to manage: find the sign, settle into the rhythm, stop negotiating with your watch. Before you join, check what that sign actually promises. The same finish-time target can hide different plans for the start, hills and water stops.

A pace group is useful when its target fits your training and its way of getting there suits you. Keep your own elapsed time and checkpoints as a backup. You can leave a group without having failed your race.

What does a marathon pacer do?

A mass-participation marathon pacer leads runners towards an advertised finish time. The leader usually carries a sign or another visible marker, monitors the pace and provides encouragement. This guide concerns those public groups, rather than the athletes hired to pace the front of an elite race.

The details belong to the event. Check its current pace-team page for available finish times, corral access, registration and whether the leader runs the whole distance. A balloon at the start does not give you permission to enter another corral.

For example, Chicago’s official pace-team guidance specifies chip timing and limits runners to groups accessible from their assigned corral. Those are useful details to look for at your own race; they are not universal event rules. Check the information for the edition you entered.

A group’s finish-time sign is also different from a course cutoff. The event may apply separate deadlines to intermediate points or use a different clock for course closure. Find those rules before deciding a group will keep you within them.

How do you choose between two pace groups?

Choose from the finish time your recent training supports. If your evidence suggests roughly 4:10 and the choices are 4:00 or 4:15, joining 4:00 because it is the next sign available asks you to run a different race. You could use the 4:15 group for company early, then follow your own plan, or run independently from the start.

Use the goal-time guide to weigh recent races, long-run execution and the build you actually completed. The marathon predictor supplies estimates to discuss, not a pass to follow a faster group.

For a four-hour target, the exact average is about 5:41.3/km or 9:09.2/mile. Three hours fifty-five minutes means about 5:34.6/km. That difference is only about seven seconds per kilometre on paper, but you would be choosing to hold it for 42.195 km. Small-looking pace differences deserve a proper decision before the gun.

Think about the group’s movement as well as its speed. If you have rehearsed regular walk breaks, ask whether it uses the same pattern. A continuous group that walks briefly for drinks is different from your planned run-walk intervals. Work through the run-walk pacing examples before assuming the same finish time makes the strategies interchangeable.

Chip time works best when you cross the start together

Gun time runs from the official start signal. Chip or net time runs from your passage over the start mat to your passage over the finish mat. A wave can have its own start signal. Confirm which reference your pacer uses, then start your elapsed timer as you cross the line.

Austin’s pace-team page shows why checking matters: it currently specifies gun time for the 3:00 group and chip time for the other groups. Confirm the instructions for your own event and edition.

There is a second question: did you cross with the pacer? Suppose a pacer crosses at 09:05:00 and finishes at 13:05:00. That is four hours of chip time. If you cross at 09:07:00 and eventually finish beside them, your chip time is 3:58:00. If you cross at 09:03:00 and finish beside them, yours is 4:02:00. Following the same sign later does not make the starting offsets disappear.

When you finish together: your chip time = pacer’s chip time + pacer’s start time − your start time.

This is clock arithmetic, not a reason to force your way forward at the start. Line up legally, cross near the group if possible, and know your own elapsed target if you join later. A pace band gives you cumulative checkpoints that remain useful when the sign is out of sight.

Ask whether the sign means exactly the target or a small margin beneath it. If a pacer plans 3:59 for a four-hour sign, your comparison should use 3:59. There is no standard amount you should assume they will “bank”.

Lost the group? Calculate the catch-up pace first

Thirty seconds behind a group can feel like an emergency. The pace needed to erase that gap depends on how much road you allow. This worksheet assumes the group keeps a constant pace and the gap is measured at the same course marker.

Use your elapsed-time difference at one marker, not a guess from how far away the sign looks. This calculates a pace; it does not recommend a chase.

Group’s average pace
Required catch-up pace

Worked example: a four-hour group averages 341.27 seconds/km. To recover 30 seconds over the next 2 km, you need to average 326.27 seconds/km, about 5:26/km. Recovering the same gap over 5 km requires about 5:35/km. Both figures assume the group holds its average pace and you have that distance available.

The equation is group pace − gap ÷ catch-up distance. It cannot tell you whether that acceleration is sensible after a toilet stop, in heat or late in a marathon. If the required pace is harder than you intended to run, follow your own checkpoints. A distant sign is not evidence that your race is going badly. Read the time-in-the-bank calculation before trading a small delay for a much harder effort.

How to stay with a group without surrendering your race

At the start

Leave enough room to run naturally. You do not need to sit on the pacer’s shoulder. Avoid repeated weaving to keep the sign perfectly centred. If congestion creates a slow kilometre, find out whether the leader intends to recover the delay gradually; do not add a separate surge of your own.

At aid stations

Ask whether the group runs through, walks briefly or spreads across the tables and regroups. Get your own drink and take your planned fuel. Passing a bottle because you are afraid of losing a balloon defeats the reason you practised the routine. Move aside if stopping, and avoid cutting across another runner.

On hills or into wind

A steady elapsed schedule and a steady effort can give different kilometre splits. Ask which the leader prioritises. Use the conditions guide to decide when your intended effort needs to take precedence. If the pace already feels unsustainable, dropping behind early gives you more room to adjust than waiting until a forced stop.

If the pacer disappears or slows

Check your elapsed time at the next official marker, compare it with your band and continue the plan. Do not diagnose a pacing error from a single live GPS reading. The GPS pacing guide explains why watch distance and course markers can disagree. Pacers can also have a bad day; keep the information needed to run independently.

Seven questions to take to the pace-team stand

  1. Which clock do you use, and what exact finish time are you aiming for?
  2. Can I access this group from my assigned corral?
  3. How will you handle congestion in the opening kilometres?
  4. Do you run evenly by distance or adjust pace for hills?
  5. Do you walk through drinks, and where do you regroup?
  6. Does the leader stay for the full distance? What happens if a leader stops?
  7. What should I do if I lose contact or need a longer stop?

Make the final choice after you have those answers. The useful group is the one whose plan you understand and can follow at an appropriate effort.
