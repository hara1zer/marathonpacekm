Strength training for marathon runners: two short gym sessions

By Davin Pinto · Published 25 October 2026 · Research and worked examples

Two short gym sessions can fit around marathon training. The difficult part is keeping them useful when the running gets harder, the gym is busy and you have thirty minutes before work.

The sessions below cover a squat or step-up, a hip hinge or hamstring exercise, calf work and a small amount of trunk or upper-body work. Each comes with substitutions, a time budget and a way to record progression. They are examples for healthy adults who can perform the movements comfortably, rather than an injury rehabilitation programme.

What does strength training do for marathon runners?

A 2024 systematic review by Llanos-Lagos and colleagues found that some strength methods, including high-load and combined approaches, can improve running economy. Economy describes the energy required at a given running speed. Effects varied with the training method and runners studied.

That supports strength as part of a running programme. It does not establish that these exact thirty-minute sessions will remove a certain number of minutes from your marathon. The 2026 ACSM resistance-training position stand also supports strength training's general benefits in healthy adults; it is broader than marathon-specific performance research.

Performance benefits and injury prevention are different claims. A 2024 review of exercise-based injury prevention in runners found a mixed picture, with limitations in the evidence. These sessions therefore do not come with an “injury-proof” promise.

Start with a load you can control

Use a light practice set to find a comfortable movement and range. For the working sets, choose a load that lets you complete every repetition cleanly while feeling you could do another two or three. That is the meaning of 2–3 repetitions in reserve here. You do not need a one-repetition maximum test.

If you are new to lifting or returning after a long gap, do just one working set of each exercise for the first few exposures and review the response. The full two-set sheets are an option once that fits your recovery. Start during a relatively manageable running period. Ask gym staff or a qualified coach to check unfamiliar movements and machine setup.

Rest roughly 90–120 seconds between squat, hinge and step-up sets; around 60–90 seconds can work for the smaller exercises. Take longer when you need it. The timetable includes setup and practice sets, but busy equipment can use that allowance. Leave out the final accessory if time runs short.

Reference: marathonpacekm.com/blog/strength-training-for-marathon-runners/. Record the machine or exercise variant as well as the load; different machines' weight stacks are not directly comparable.

Session A: squat, hinge and straight-knee calf raise

Allow about 30 minutes. Two working sets are shown; use the one-set introduction above when appropriate.

Session B: step-up, hamstrings and bent-knee calf work

Allow about 30 minutes. Keep the exercises familiar enough that setup does not consume the session.

  1. 0–5 minutes: warm up. Easy bike or walking, followed by low unloaded step-ups and light practice repetitions on the first machine.
  2. 5–13 minutes: low step-up, 2 × 6 per leg. Choose a stable step low enough to rise without pushing hard from the trailing foot. Keep a support nearby if needed and lower under control. Where should strength go in a marathon training week?

Place the long run and any established quality session first. Then look for two strength slots that leave enough recovery before those priorities. Pairing lifting with a run day can preserve full rest days, but it also creates a larger day of work. There is no placement that suits every shift pattern or recovery response.

Running frequencyExample placementWhat to review
3 days: Tue, Thu, SunSession A after Tuesday's run; B after Thursday's run.Monday, Wednesday, Friday and Saturday can remain free of running and lifting. Check Thursday's work is recovered from before Sunday.
4 days: Tue, Wed, Fri, SunSession A after Tuesday; B after Friday's run.Keep Friday's work familiar and manageable. If it compromises Sunday's long run, reduce it, move it or use one strength session.
5 days: Tue, Wed, Thu, Sat, SunSession A Tuesday; B Thursday.If Tuesday includes a key running workout, complete the run first and separate sessions when practical. Reduce total load if the combination is too much.

Check the response to the placement, especially when the long run grows. Start with one weekly strength session if the second slot consistently harms the running or leaves you under-recovered.

If you do both on one day, putting the priority run first is a practical way to avoid beginning it with fatigued legs. This is a scheduling choice, not a proven ideal order for every training goal. Use the monthly planner for the run calendar and review the gym work alongside it.

How to make progress without adding more exercises

Keep a small record: exercise, load, repetitions, repetitions left and next-day response. When both sets reach the top of the range with steady technique, two or three repetitions still available and acceptable recovery, try the smallest useful load increase next time. Return towards the lower end of the range.

For example, a runner might move from 2 × 6 to 2 × 8 on the same leg press before increasing the weight. That is an illustration of progression, not a requirement to progress every week. If the next machine increment is large, keep the old load and improve consistency instead.

Do not use soreness as the score. If normal walking, stairs or the next planned easy run is meaningfully disrupted, reduce the next session's sets or load and review the schedule. Pain that is sharp, focal, worsening or changes movement needs assessment rather than progression.

The introductory loads here are for learning and tolerating the routine. They do not reproduce the high-load interventions in running-economy research. Progressing to heavier work requires suitable technique, preparation and recovery; doing more repetitions with a very light weight is not the same intervention.

Adjust the gym work as the race approaches

As running demands rise, review the strength volume. Retaining one or two familiar exercises with fewer sets can be more workable than completing the whole sheet. Avoid introducing plyometrics or a new heavy lift simply because race day is close.

During the marathon taper, preserve the intended reduction in overall work. Previous responses to lifting matter more than finishing a printed checklist. Someone who reliably gets sore from a session needs a different final-week decision from someone well accustomed to it.

Strength work also needs recovery and adequate food. The weight-loss and marathon-training guide explains the competing demands when a runner is also trying to lose weight. Use the training-duration guide to leave room for establishing the routine before the hardest running weeks.

Marathon strength training questions

Are machines or free weights better for a returning lifter?

Either can provide resistance. Choose a setup you can use comfortably and consistently. A machine may make setup and balance easier; free weights require control of a less constrained movement. The programme does not require a barbell squat.

Do runners need jump training?

Plyometrics appear in running-economy research, but they add another training demand. They are not required to begin these sessions. Learn and tolerate the basic routine first; individual preparation matters.

Can I finish in twenty minutes?

Keep the warm-up and first two exercises, then calf work if time allows. Leave out the final trunk or upper-body accessory. Do not save time by rushing loaded repetitions or eliminating needed rest.

Sources and programme scope

  • Llanos-Lagos C and colleagues (2024), Effect of Strength Training Programs in Middle- and Long-Distance Runners' Economy at Different Running Speeds. Systematic review and meta-analysis of running-economy outcomes.
  • Currier BS and colleagues (2026), ACSM Position Stand: Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults. General adult resistance-training evidence.
  • Wu H and colleagues (2024), Do Exercise-Based Prevention Programs Reduce Injury in Endurance Runners? Injury prevention is a separate outcome with different evidence.

The exercise sheets and scheduling examples were assembled for this article. They are not a trial-tested protocol, a personal success story or a substitute for individual coaching. Davin Pinto writes as a recreational runner and site creator.
