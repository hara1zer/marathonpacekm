Pacing decisions

How to recognise possible marathon overpacing

Compare what you are doing with the pace and effort you prepared for. Comfortable early running can be completely appropriate; the concern is a mismatch between the plan, conditions and effort.

By Davin Pinto · Updated 1 October 2026 · Editorial policy · Corrections

Start with the planned pace

Suppose your even four-hour plan averages 5:41.3/km. Running the first five kilometres at 5:20/km puts you about 1:46 ahead. That is an observable change in the plan, even if it feels easy. Running near the planned pace and feeling comfortable does not provide the same evidence.

Use the checkpoint guide to compare cumulative times rather than interpreting one fluctuating GPS reading.

Look for a combination of signals

  • Your checkpoints are repeatedly faster than planned on comparable terrain.
  • Breathing and perceived effort are substantially harder than in relevant training.
  • You are repeatedly surging after bends, crowds or hills.
  • A credible heart-rate trend is unusually high for the effort and conditions you know.

None of these measures is perfect. Heart rate can be affected by conditions or sensor error, and the course may explain a split change. Check an unexpected heart-rate reading before treating it as a diagnosis.

Respond without creating a second problem

Ease to a more manageable effort, stop unnecessary surges, and compare the next suitable section with your plan. Do not assume the original pace must remain appropriate if heat or terrain has changed. A small pace reduction can be a useful trial when otherwise well, but no fixed seconds-per-kilometre adjustment guarantees a recovery.

If you have severe or unusual symptoms, stop and seek assistance rather than continuing a pacing experiment. See the mid-race adjustment guide for ordinary performance decisions.

What my two halves can and cannot show

My first five recorded kilometres in 2025 averaged 5:33.4/km; kilometres 16–20 averaged 6:24.2/km. In 2026, the corresponding averages were 5:15.4 and 5:08.6/km. The comparison shows why I now care about finishing strength as well as speed.

That pattern does not prove an exact sustainable opening pace or establish why I faded in 2025. GPS distance, terrain and unrecorded differences limit the comparison.

Review the goal after the race

Compare your plan, recorded splits, effort notes and conditions. Keep a controllable change for the next attempt: for example, avoid an unplanned early acceleration. Use goal selection to revisit the target itself, not just your discipline.

Common questions

Does feeling unusually good mean I am overpacing?

No. Comfortable early effort can be appropriate. Check whether you are running faster than your prepared plan and whether other signs suggest the pace is becoming costly.

Can I regain lost seconds immediately?

A sudden catch-up changes effort again. First settle and reassess the target; the remaining distance calculation can show what preserving it would require.

Is heart rate enough to decide?

No. Consider measurement quality, your own comparable training, symptoms, terrain, weather and perceived effort.
