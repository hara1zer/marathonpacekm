Marathon pace readiness: how to review your training

No single workout or homemade score can certify that you will hold a pace for 42.195 km. Review evidence from your existing training and use it to choose a target you can explain.

What you are trying to learn

Separate three questions: can you run the proposed pace comfortably enough in an appropriate session, can you sustain your training without worsening symptoms or persistent exhaustion, and have you practised the practical demands of a longer race? These overlap, but none can be reduced to one split or heart-rate number.

Review before adding another test

Start with sessions already completed. Near race day, an extra hard long run can cost more recovery than the information is worth. If you are returning from illness or injury, resolve that first; do not use a pace test as a clearance test. Timing and workload should fit your established plan and individual recovery.

Five ways to use sessions you already have

Choose the observations relevant to your programme. The examples below are alternatives, not a checklist of workouts to complete. Keep total running and intensity within what you already tolerate.

1. Review a familiar long run

Look at the middle and later sections alongside the route profile. Did pace change because of hills, planned stops or rising effort? A fast finish is not compulsory. Record how you felt later that day and during subsequent easy running; finishing the session is only part of the evidence.

2. Review broken marathon-pace work

Shorter blocks can show how the pace feels without requiring a long continuous effort. For a runner already doing this work, an example is 2 km easy + 3 × 2 km at proposed marathon pace, with 1 km easy between blocks + 2 km easy: 12 km total, 6 km at pace. This is an arithmetic example, not an entry requirement or a recommendation for a returning runner.

3. Review a continuous segment

If your plan includes continuous marathon-pace running, compare its early and late effort. Do not lengthen the segment just to obtain a reassuring result. A short successful segment establishes that you handled that segment; it does not measure the remaining marathon distance.

4. Review a recent race

A recent half marathon gives more sustained racing information than a short repetition session. Look beyond the finish figure to the course and split pattern. My two Run Melbourne halves show a late fade in 2025 and a stronger finish in 2026, but they do not explain which training changes caused that difference.

5. Compare repeatable easy runs

On a familiar route, record pace, heart rate, perceived effort and conditions. Several comparable runs are more informative than one unusually good day. Heart-rate drift can reflect prolonged exercise, heat, hydration and other influences; a drift percentage is not a pass/fail test for marathon readiness.

Keep the comparison fair

  • Use your normal preparation and familiar equipment.
  • Record weather, elevation, stops and fuel rather than comparing pace alone.
  • Do not force the target through worsening pain or illness.
  • Count warm-up, recoveries and cool-down in the total workload.

How to interpret mixed evidence

If the target repeatedly demands more effort than intended in comparable sessions, reconsider it with your coach or plan. If one session was hot or poorly recovered, reduce its weight in the decision. Comfortable sessions support the target but do not guarantee it. More data can also reveal that the target needs to be slower.

Respond to the specific gap

Uncertain fuel calls for a practical nutrition review; an overly ambitious goal calls for revising the goal. Neither automatically calls for more kilometres. Persistent fatigue may call for less training and a health assessment. Avoid trying to fix every uncertainty with another demanding long run.

Questions about readiness

Is there a workout that proves my goal?

No. Workouts provide evidence under their own conditions; the marathon adds duration and race-day uncertainty.

Do I need to run 30–35 km with long pace blocks?

No universal session is required. That workload may be inappropriate for your training history, pace, recovery or health.

What should I do after a poor session?

Review conditions and recovery, address symptoms, and look at the broader pattern before choosing the next session. Do not immediately repeat the test.

Sources and limits

  • Hunter and colleagues (2025), Durability of parameters associated with endurance running in marathoners
  • Halson (2014), Monitoring training load to understand fatigue in athletes
  • Coyle and González-Alonso (2001), Cardiovascular drift during prolonged exercise
  • Blythe and Király (2016), Prediction and quantification of individual athletic performance of runners

Research supports considering fatigue, training context and the limits of predictions. It does not validate this review sheet or any individual workout as a marathon pass mark.

What my Sydney build taught me

My build contained an 83.9 km peak week, but the 14-week average was about 60.7 km. Treating the peak as my normal workload would have overstated my preparation. I also completed pace sessions and still missed my sub-four goal. Read the recorded training and race review. The lesson is to consider the whole block, not to replace one universal threshold with another.
