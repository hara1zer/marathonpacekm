My Sydney Marathon: lessons from a sub-four attempt

By Davin Pinto · Written 21 September 2026 · Updated 22 September 2026 · Personal race review

I started running in late 2024 with the goal of finishing a half marathon. Less than two years later, I was preparing for Sydney.

I went into Sydney Marathon with a 1:50:57 half marathon behind me and a sub-four goal. I missed that goal. Looking back, I wanted to understand how my preparation and race execution needed to improve.

My main lessons were about consistent training, keeping marathon-specific work controlled and practising the full race-day plan. This is a review of those choices and what I want to do differently next time.

The part of the race I need to improve

The later kilometres became a cycle of running and walking. I had the speed for a strong half marathon, but I did not maintain the effort I wanted over the full marathon.

My practical change: put more weight on repeatable long-run effort and recovery when choosing a target. The pace calculator can give me checkpoints; my training has to support the plan.

What my half marathon predicted—and what happened

The simple Riegel conversion multiplies the half-marathon time by two raised to an exponent. With 1:50:57 and exponent 1.06, the result is approximately 3:51:19. Changing the exponent to 1.08 or 1.10 produces roughly 3:54:33 or 3:57:50.

All three scenarios suggested a sub-four marathon, which I did not achieve at Sydney. The projection gave me a possible target; my preparation and execution still had to support it.

My shorter-race result supported considering sub-four. It did not establish the durability, pacing and fueling needed to achieve it. The predictor now makes that distinction explicit: its exponents show different assumptions, not personal confidence limits.

My training peak was not my normal week

My Strava history puts the final build in context. Across the 12 complete Monday-to-Sunday weeks from 1 June to 23 August 2026, I averaged 60.7 km and 4.4 runs per week. My biggest week was 83.9 km, but that was a peak rather than a settled baseline.

That context matters: I was trying to build fitness while managing interruptions, rather than deliberately alternating very high and very low mileage. It also makes staying healthy and stringing together repeatable weeks an important part of my next build.

The final three full weeks were 74.2, 54.3 and 46.4 km. I then ran another 20.5 km during race week before the marathon. These figures include the taper; they are a description of what I did, not a mileage prescription for another runner.

My practical change: plan from the training I can repeat, including low and zero weeks, rather than treating my best week as my normal load. The monthly planner includes all four entered weeks in its average.

A workout label did not make the effort marathon-specific

My July half-marathon pace was about 5:16/km. A 28 July workout labelled “3 × 3 km MP” had blocks at approximately 4:43, 4:50 and 4:57/km, with long recoveries. A 5 August run included 10 km labelled marathon pace at about 5:15/km.

Those sessions showed an ability to do faster work. They did not prove I could hold that pace for 42.195 km. In the latter session, the designated marathon pace was essentially my recent half-marathon race pace.

My practical change: record the intended effort, actual pace, recovery time and next-day response. A controlled marathon-pace rehearsal should be judged in the context of the whole training block, rather than counted as successful because I finished the repetitions. It became difficult before I started walking

The race started feeling difficult a little before 30 km. I first walked at roughly 31–32 km. Earlier, I had a mild stitch that I could manage with controlled breathing; when the bigger slowdown came, I was not sore. The best description of the feeling was empty.

My pace had eased before the first walking break. Once walking started, it became repeated running and walking through the later kilometres. I could still accelerate near the finish, but I had not sustained the effort I wanted over the distance.

I experienced a physical sense of depletion, and I think there may have been a psychological element too. The useful lesson for me is to recognise that change earlier and practise how I respond, rather than reduce the whole race to a question of willpower.

Taking more gels became difficult when I started to fade

I used Winners gels at Sydney, aiming to take one roughly every 20 minutes. Around the same time the race became difficult, my stomach no longer felt like taking more. That made the later part of the race harder to manage: I felt empty, but eating another gel was becoming unappealing.

For me, the lesson is that a planned schedule has to be something I can actually follow late in a long run. My earlier experiments with homemade gels had also been difficult to take, with a dry mouth. Next time I want to rehearse the products, timing and drinks together, including how they feel after several hours.

My practical change: check the carbohydrate amount on the exact product, record what I actually consume and include carbohydrate from drinks. The fueling calculator compares a selected schedule with an hourly target; I still need to practise that schedule. The timing of my symptoms is part of my experience, not proof that a particular carbohydrate shortfall caused the slowdown.

The 32 km rehearsal was another lesson in finishing well

My 9 August long run covered 32.0 km. The recorded two-kilometre laps show the first 10 km at roughly 5:16–5:31/km. By the final two full laps, the pace had eased to about 6:28/km and 6:47/km.

Completing the distance gave me useful experience, but the way I finished mattered too. For my next build, I want to judge a rehearsal by how controlled it feels through the later kilometres and whether I can keep taking fuel, not just by reaching the planned distance.

What I am taking into the next build

  • Use consistent mileage as the baseline, rather than my best week.
  • Keep marathon rehearsals distinct from faster threshold or half-marathon work.
  • Choose a goal supported by recent racing, repeatable training and long-run recovery.
  • Practise fueling and course-specific effort without assuming a perfect race day.
  • Check effort at course markers and revise the target when it no longer fits.

These are lessons from one runner's experience. They do not prescribe another runner's ideal mileage or explain every late-marathon slowdown.
