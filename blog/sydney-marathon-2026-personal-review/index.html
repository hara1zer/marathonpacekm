My Sydney Marathon: the gap between a 1:50 half and 4:14 elapsed

By Davin Pinto · Written 21 September 2026 · Personal race review

My Sydney Marathon watch recorded 4:14:10 elapsed. Before the race, a 1:50:57 half marathon made sub-four look plausible on a calculator. The useful question afterwards was why I could run the shorter distance well but could not carry that performance through the marathon.

The review points to a gap between shorter-race fitness and sustained marathon effort. It does not establish one measured cause, a precise overpacing penalty or a guaranteed faster result with a different strategy. Here is what I can take from the recorded numbers.

First, use the right finish-time measure

The watch showed about 5:55/km using its timer and GPS distance. Dividing 4:14:10 by the standard 42.195 km instead gives about 6:01/km. Neither calculation replaces official chip timing, which was not available in my review.

The 3:59:57 run-time field was especially easy to misread. Running for just under four hours within a longer race is not a sub-four marathon. Likewise, walking time is not automatically time lost: I was still covering distance, and those breaks may have helped me continue.

My practical change: compare continuous elapsed time with official course markers. Keep pauses in the race plan. The pace calculator gives elapsed-time checkpoints for that purpose.

What my half marathon predicted—and what happened

The simple Riegel conversion multiplies the half-marathon time by two raised to an exponent. With 1:50:57 and exponent 1.06, the result is approximately 3:51:19. Changing the exponent to 1.08 or 1.10 produces roughly 3:54:33 or 3:57:50.

Those are mathematical scenarios. My 4:14:10 watch elapsed time was around 16–23 minutes slower. That difference is a reason to examine preparation and execution, not a reserve of minutes I can simply claim back next time.

My shorter-race result supported considering sub-four. It did not establish the durability, pacing and fueling needed to achieve it. The predictor now makes that distinction explicit: its exponents show different assumptions, not personal confidence limits.

My training peak was not my normal week

The report calculated a 58.8 km weekly average over the 12 complete weeks from 11 May to 2 August. A shorter four-week block averaged 73.3 km, but only four of the 12 weeks reached 70 km.

That sequence averages about 59 km despite two weeks over 80 km. Planning the next cycle as if 80 km were an established base would ignore the lower weeks. The final 24 pre-race days were missing from the available training dataset, so this is not a complete account of the build.

My practical change: plan from repeatable training, including low and zero weeks. The monthly planner includes all four entered weeks in its average.

A workout label did not make the effort marathon-specific

My July half-marathon pace was about 5:16/km. A 28 July workout labelled “3 × 3 km MP” had blocks at approximately 4:43, 4:50 and 4:57/km, with long recoveries. A 5 August run included 10 km labelled marathon pace at about 5:15/km.

Those sessions showed an ability to do faster work. They did not prove I could hold that pace for 42.195 km. In the latter session, the designated marathon pace was essentially my recent half-marathon race pace.

My practical change: record the intended effort, actual pace, recovery time and next-day response. A controlled marathon-pace rehearsal should be judged in the context of the whole training block, rather than counted as successful because I finished the repetitions. See tempo, threshold and marathon pace.

The slowing started before repeated walking

The report's approximate readings of the watch graphs describe rising heart rate and softening output before repeated walk/run troughs appeared around 2:50–3:00 on the timer axis. The original streams were not available for this article, so I cannot supply exact official splits or a reliable cardiac-drift percentage.

A brief acceleration near the finish does not show that the same output was sustainable for the preceding hour. Terrain, shorter remaining duration and the recovery from walking may all matter. I cannot apportion the lost minutes between them.

The strongest interpretation in the report was limited sustained endurance alongside costly pacing in training. Fueling, fluid intake and terrain remained plausible contributors. That is more useful than treating the walking as a single failure of willpower.

“A gel every 20 minutes” was not a complete fueling record

I reported gels roughly every 20 minutes during the race and an empty feeling later. My training account also included difficulty taking homemade gels and dry mouth. The actual race carbohydrate grams and fluid volume were not measured in the available records.

That leaves a real uncertainty. A schedule cannot establish how much carbohydrate I consumed or absorbed, and feeling empty is not a direct glycogen measurement. I cannot conclude that too few gels caused the slowdown.

My practical change: rehearse the whole menu, check grams on each product, record what I actually consume and include carbohydrate from drinks. The fueling calculator now compares the selected schedule with the hourly target. It does not count backup gels as eaten.

What I am taking into the next build

  • Use consistent mileage as the baseline, rather than my best week.
  • Keep marathon rehearsals distinct from faster threshold or half-marathon work.
  • Choose a goal supported by recent racing, repeatable training and long-run recovery.
  • Practise fueling and course-specific effort without assuming a perfect race day.
  • Track elapsed time and revise a target when the effort no longer fits.

These are lessons from one runner's experience. They do not prescribe another runner's ideal mileage or explain every late-marathon slowdown.

Source and limits of this review

The race and training figures come from my supplied performance review dated 5 September 2026, which summarised watch screenshots and a training-log analysis. The underlying screenshots, full race stream and official course splits were not independently reanalysed for this article. Reported sensations are personal recollections; proposed causes are interpretations. I have not published the full personal training document.

The broader distinction between training consistency and a one-off peak is consistent with Zanini and colleagues' observational study of training and running-economy durability; it does not prove what caused my result.
