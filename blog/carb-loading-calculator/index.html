Carb-loading calculator: turn a daily target into a food plan

By Davin Pinto · Published 25 October 2026 · Research and worked examples

A daily carbohydrate target is easier to calculate than to eat. The useful next step is a food plan: what you will have, how much carbohydrate its label provides, and what you actually manage to consume. This worksheet keeps those three records separate.

Enter a target you have chosen with appropriate guidance. The calculator converts it into grams per day; the food worksheet helps you build and check one day’s plan. It does not assess your nutritional needs or measure how much glycogen you store.

How much carbohydrate does carb loading mean?

The 2019 IAAF nutrition consensus describes carbohydrate loading of 10–12 g per kilogram of body mass per day for 36–48 hours before events lasting longer than about 90 minutes. This is a sports-nutrition strategy for increasing pre-event carbohydrate availability, alongside reduced training. It is not a general everyday eating target.

The “per day” matters. A target of 700 g/day does not mean 700 g across a whole two-day window, or 700 g added on top of your usual food. It means the carbohydrate total for the day. A meal you already intended to eat contributes to that total.

The calculator lets you enter a different g/kg target because an individual plan needs context, including health, event duration, tolerance and food access. Its input limits prevent nonsensical arithmetic; they do not mark every accepted value as suitable. The default 70 kg and 10 g/kg are an example, not an assessment of you.

If you have diabetes, a condition requiring a prescribed diet, or a history that makes food or weight tracking difficult, use a sports dietitian or your treating clinician to plan this. The author is a recreational runner, not a dietitian, and this worksheet has not received an individual clinical review.

Your food entries

Target example: 70 kg × 10 g/kg/day = 700 g/day. Two complete days at that daily target would total 1,400 g across those days. The tool shows one day at a time: download its record before resetting or reloading the page for the next day.

Food example using a hypothetical label: a product provides 30 g carbohydrate per stated serving. Planning two servings gives 60 g. If you record consuming 1.5 servings, the recorded amount is 45 g. The difference is 15 g; the plan and actual intake are no longer identical. These numbers demonstrate the calculation, not the nutrition of a named product.

Carbohydrate grams are different from food grams

Weighing 100 g of a food does not mean you are eating 100 g of carbohydrate. Use the nutrition panel’s carbohydrate figure for the portion you will actually consume. Write the portion unit beside the food so you can reproduce it.

Per-serving label: carbohydrate per serving × servings consumed.
Per-100-g label: carbohydrate per 100 g × food weight eaten ÷ 100.

For another hypothetical example, a label gives 24 g carbohydrate per 100 g of food. A 150 g portion contributes 36 g carbohydrate. In the worksheet you could define the serving as “150 g weighed portion”, enter 36 g per serving, then plan one serving. Or define it as “100 g” and enter 24 g with 1.5 planned servings. Both approaches give the same answer.

Keep preparation state consistent. A nutrition panel for dry rice or pasta should not be applied directly to a cooked portion’s weight. Water changes the food’s mass during cooking. Use the label as directed, weigh the corresponding state or calculate the whole recipe and divide by the portions actually made.

Check whether a drink’s numbers refer to the concentrate, prepared drink, one scoop or a whole bottle. Count the amount consumed, including any remainder left in the bottle. Use the carbohydrate line rather than counting only the “sugars” line. If labels from different countries or fibre accounting make the target unclear, ask your dietitian which convention to use.

The FDA’s label guide explains why the stated serving size and number of servings per container matter. A manufacturer’s serving is a reporting unit; it is not an individual recommendation for how much to eat.

Turn the daily target into meals you can actually organise

Begin with familiar foods and the places where you will be eating. Race travel changes access: a hotel breakfast, an early checkout or a restaurant menu can make a beautifully calculated plan impractical. Buy or pack the specific foods you have counted, and check portion sizes before the last evening.

As a planning exercise, a 700 g day could be divided into six carbohydrate budgets of 140, 70, 160, 80, 160 and 90 g. They add to 700. Those are allocation slots, not a complete menu or a recommendation that any one meal should contain that amount. Replace the slots with actual foods and label-based quantities you can tolerate.

For breakfast, that might mean working out the portions of a familiar cereal or bread, its usual accompaniments and a drink. At lunch and dinner, identify the carbohydrate-containing part of the meal and its preparation state. Use snacks to make the day workable, rather than expecting dinner to solve every missing gram.

Retain the rest of a sensible eating plan. The worksheet counts carbohydrate only; it does not check energy, protein, fat, fibre, micronutrients or fluid needs. A green-looking total is not a complete nutritional assessment. There is no need to buy gels to fill every food entry.

The Australian Institute of Sport’s sports-food guidance describes lower-fibre carbohydrate options as useful in some pre-exercise situations. That does not make wholesale food restriction necessary. Rehearse any changes, including a lower-fibre selection, instead of inventing a new diet in race week.

Keep carb loading, race breakfast and on-course fuel separate

These are different planning jobs. Loading concerns the day or days before the event. Breakfast concerns what you eat before the start. On-course fueling concerns what you carry, collect and consume during the race.

This worksheet’s target is grams per day. The marathon fueling calculator works with grams per hour during running. Do not enter the daily loading total as a race-fueling rate, or count a breakfast twice because it appears on two lists.

Loading is not permission to skip your rehearsed race fuel. Equally, taking extra gels on the start line does not recreate the previous day’s plan. Use the fueling-by-finish-time guide for the separate problem of carrying and timing intake over a longer race.

Fit the food plan around the taper, travel and start time. Do not add a depletion workout to make yourself “deserve” the extra food. If you are uncertain about the loading window, have a sports dietitian translate the guideline into actual dates and meals for your event.

Rehearse the food plan and record the result

Test the practical routine before a suitable training rehearsal, in agreement with the plan you follow. Record the food, label, portion, timing and comfort. The point is to learn whether you can buy, prepare and eat the plan, not to prove a particular glycogen level from how full you feel.

My published fueling experiments concern intake during running, rather than a controlled carb-loading trial. They nevertheless exposed a record-keeping problem: intended intake and consumed intake are different claims. I have not used those recollections to invent a successful loading menu for this article.
