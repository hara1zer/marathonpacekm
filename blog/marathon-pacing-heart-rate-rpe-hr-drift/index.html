Why HR + RPE are useful for marathon pacing

Pace is external. Heart rate and RPE are internal. The marathon is long enough that internal cost matters more than early pace precision. Research in marathoners has linked pacing strategy to “cardiac drift” and performance, and “cardiac cost” (heart rate relative to running speed) has been used to quantify how the internal cost of the same pace changes during a marathon.

Core idea

Pace is a plan. Effort is the truth. HR and RPE stop you from spending effort too early—especially when conditions or adrenaline trick you.

HR isn’t perfect (it lags and it drifts), and RPE is subjective. But together they give you a reliable “effort dashboard” when GPS and crowd chaos are unreliable.

What RPE is (and how to use it)

RPE (rating of perceived exertion) is a simple way to score how hard the work feels. The classic Borg scale runs from 6 to 20 and was designed to roughly correlate with heart rate (in broad strokes), and many studies show RPE correlates strongly with physiological markers like heart rate and lactate.

RPE (0–10) What it feels like Talk test Where it fits in marathon day
3–4 Easy / relaxed Full sentences Warm-up & early settling; easy kilometres
5 Steady / “all day” Short sentences Early marathon once settled (cool conditions)
6 Controlled work Short phrases Typical marathon effort mid-race
7 Hard but manageable 1–3 words Late marathon if pacing/fueling are good
8+ Very hard / survival Not really Final push only (last few km)

RPE guardrail

If you’re at “threshold feel” (RPE 7–8, talk test broken) before 5–10K, you’re either pacing too fast or conditions are harsher than your plan assumed. Fix it early.

Why heart rate drifts upward (even at the same pace)

During prolonged exercise, many runners see HR slowly creep upward even while pace stays steady. This is commonly described as cardiovascular drift. Reviews describe the pattern as a time-dependent rise in HR alongside declines in stroke volume and mean arterial pressure during prolonged work.

The main drivers of HR drift

  • Heat stress / thermoregulation: as skin and core temperature rise, cardiovascular demands shift, which can reduce stroke volume and push HR up.
  • Dehydration and reduced plasma volume: fluid loss can reduce stroke volume and cardiac output, especially in the heat, increasing HR for the same pace.
  • Reduced ventricular filling time at higher HR: as HR rises, filling time shortens, which can further reduce stroke volume.
  • Neuromuscular fatigue: as mechanics degrade slightly, the same pace can become “costlier.”

Why drift matters for pacing

If you pace only by HR, drift can force you to slow more than necessary late. If you pace only by pace, drift can hide that you’re overcooking the internal cost early. That’s why marathon pacing works best as a hybrid.

Marathon research specifically has linked pacing strategy with cardiac drift and performance, and more recent work in elite marathoners discusses how stable HR early in the marathon can relate to performance and “durability.”

The best approach: hybrid pacing (pace + HR cap + RPE)

Here’s the simplest way to think about it:

When Primary guide Secondary guide Why
0–10K HR cap RPE + pace cap Adrenaline + crowds make pace feel easy; HR cap prevents early debt
10–30K Pace (if conditions stable) RPE Settle into sustainable rhythm; keep effort honest
30–42K RPE HR + pace Late drift/fatigue makes HR less actionable; RPE tells you what’s sustainable

Make hybrid pacing easy

Use your goal pace + pace band as the external plan, and use conditions-adjusted expectations plus an early HR cap as the internal guardrail.

A race-day HR & RPE script (0–42.2K)

This is a “doable” script—not a perfect physiological model. The key is restraint early and patience mid-race. If you want a pace-first script for the opening, pair this with: How to pace the first 10K.

0–3K: settle (HR lags; don’t chase it)

  • RPE: 4–5 (controlled, relaxed)
  • Rule: no surges, no weaving
  • HR: ignore the first 2–3 minutes; HR response lags

3–10K: the “HR cap” window

  • RPE: 5–6 (short phrases OK)
  • HR: hold under your planned early cap (see next section for how to set it)
  • Pace: allow a slight pace float on hills/headwinds; don’t “buy” pace with effort

Early red flag

If you’re at RPE 7 (“hard”) before 10K, or HR is already near your threshold-range, you’re overpacing or conditions are harsh. Adjust now.

10–30K: lock in (pace as plan, RPE as truth)

  • RPE: 6 (steady focus)
  • Pace: target goal MP when conditions are stable
  • HR: expect gradual drift; don’t panic-slow unless RPE is rising too
  • Fuel: keep fueling on schedule (don’t wait for “low energy”)

30–42K: race by RPE (with HR as context)

  • RPE: 6.5 → 7.5 depending on your day
  • HR: may climb from drift; use it as a warning sign, not a steering wheel
  • Pace: let pace reflect the conditions and your durability; keep form cues strong

Simple finish rule

If you can still take fuel and maintain mechanics, you can push. If fueling is failing and form collapses, focus on rhythm and damage control.

How to set your HR targets without a lab test

The goal isn’t a magical HR number. The goal is a cap that keeps you honest early and supports even pacing.

Step 1: Get an accurate goal pace

Use the predictor plus your recent races, then convert to MP with the pace calculator. If it’s hot or windy, adjust with the conditions tool.

Step 2: Find your “marathon HR range” in training

In a long run with marathon-pace blocks (broken), note the HR you can hold while: (a) breathing stays controlled, (b) mechanics stay clean, (c) fueling works.

  • Example session: 2 × 6 km @ MP inside a long run
  • Record: HR average for each block + RPE start/end + drift pattern

Step 3: Set a conservative HR cap for the first 10K

Your early cap should be below the HR you held during MP blocks late in long runs. The point is restraint: prevent the “easy early” trap that turns into late-race slowdown.

Important note

Heart rate is influenced by sleep, stress, caffeine, heat, dehydration, hills, and adrenaline. That’s why RPE is always the cross-check.

How to train HR + RPE pacing in long runs

Workout 1: “HR-capped” easy long run

  • Run easy and keep HR under an easy cap
  • Observe drift: does HR creep up? does pace fall?
  • Practice fueling if the run is > 90 minutes

Workout 2: MP blocks with RPE notes (best practice)

  • Example: 28–34 km total with 2 × 6 km @ MP (2 km easy between)
  • Write down RPE at start and end of each block
  • Goal: stable pacing + controlled breathing, not “fast for Strava”

Workout 3: Progression long run by RPE

  • Start easy (RPE 4)
  • Move to steady (RPE 5)
  • Finish controlled (RPE 6), optionally near MP if the day is good

Common mistakes (and fixes)

  1. Chasing HR in the first 2K.
    Fix: HR lags. Use RPE + a pace cap early; use HR cap after you settle.
  2. Using HR as the only steering wheel late.
    Fix: drift is expected. Late-race pace should be driven by RPE + mechanics + fueling success.
  3. Ignoring conditions.
    Fix: in heat/wind/hills, anchor by RPE and let pace float; use the conditions adjuster.
  4. Turning every long run into “steady.”
    Fix: alternate easy long runs with specific long runs. Keep easy days easy.
  5. Fueling too late.
    Fix: practice fueling early and consistently; don’t wait for the crash.

FAQ

Why does my heart rate drift upward at the same pace?

Cardiovascular drift during prolonged exercise often includes a gradual decline in stroke volume and mean arterial pressure with a compensatory rise in HR. Heat stress and dehydration can amplify this effect.

Should I pace my marathon by heart rate or pace?

Hybrid works best: pace targets for stable conditions, but HR caps + RPE to prevent early overpacing and to adjust for conditions.

What RPE should a marathon feel like?

Early: controlled (short phrases). Mid: steady focus. Late: hard but manageable if pacing and fueling are right. If it feels like threshold early, you’re going too fast or conditions are harsh.

Can I use heart rate as a ceiling in the first 10K?

Yes—this is one of the simplest ways to avoid the “easy early” trap that leads to late slowdown.

How do I set my marathon HR without a lab test?

Practice marathon-pace blocks in long runs and note the HR you can hold with controlled breathing, stable mechanics, and successful fueling. Use that to set a conservative early cap on race day.
