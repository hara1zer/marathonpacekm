My races and lessons

My two Run Melbourne half marathons: from fading late to finishing strong

In 2025 I struggled late. In 2026 I finished strongly. The difference matters to me because that is what I want from my next marathon: a finish I can still run with purpose.

The difference I remember

I started running in late 2024 with the goal of completing a half marathon. Run Melbourne gave me two experiences a year apart. In July 2025, the later kilometres were a struggle. In July 2026, I had more to give near the finish.

A faster overall run is encouraging, but the way I finished is the part I want to understand and repeat. These split tables let me put that recollection beside the numbers. They also show where a simple comparison needs care.

The pace pattern behind the experience

The 2025 line generally moves towards slower paces as the run progresses. The 2026 line is steadier through much of the run before the final acceleration. Individual hills and GPS behaviour can affect a lap, so the broader pattern is more useful than one unusually quick or slow kilometre.

2025: the slowdown developed before the finish

My first two recorded kilometres were 5:24 and 5:17/km. The first five averaged 5:33.4/km. By kilometres 11–15, the average was 6:15.8/km, and kilometres 16–20 averaged 6:24.2/km. That is consistent with my memory of struggling late.

Kilometre 18 stands out at 5:45/km between slower laps. It also shows a net descent of 27 metres. I would not treat that isolated faster lap as proof that the earlier difficulty had passed.

Heart rate remained high in the later recorded laps while pace slowed. That describes the pattern; it does not diagnose its cause. The record alone cannot separate target choice, fitness, conditions and fueling.

2026: more capacity in the later kilometres

In 2026, the first three five-kilometre blocks averaged 5:15.4, 5:16.2 and 5:16.6/km. Kilometres 16–20 averaged 5:08.6/km. The final five full recorded kilometres, 17–21, averaged 5:03/km.

The final two full kilometres were 4:53 and 4:51/km. Their recorded elevation changes were −5 m and +2 m. My average heart rate for those laps rose from 167 to 174 bpm. Together with how I remember the finish, those figures show that I was able to increase effort late.

I call this a strong finish. A few faster final kilometres do not establish the exact first-half and second-half times of the race. They also do not prove that starting faster would have produced a better result.

What I take from the heart-rate difference

The activity summaries show a lower average heart rate in 2026 despite the faster recorded pace. That is an observation worth retaining, but it is not a measurement of how much fitter I became. I do not have a controlled match of conditions and sensor performance here.

The useful personal question is whether pace, perceived effort and the ability to finish well tell a consistent story. The heart-rate and effort guide explains how I use those signals without treating one number as the answer.

What I want to carry into my next marathon

My later Sydney Marathon showed the limit of carrying half-marathon confidence into a longer race. I went in with the 1:50:57 half behind me and a sub-four goal, but I did not sustain the race I wanted. I began repeated walking around 31–32 km.

The goal for my next marathon includes finishing strongly, not only arriving at a particular time. For me, that means choosing a target from repeatable training, keeping the opening controlled and rehearsing a fueling plan I can still follow late. Those are priorities for the next build, not a claim that I have already solved the marathon.

A strong half marathon is evidence of that half marathon. The next distance still needs its own preparation.

My post-Sydney rebuild intentions explain how I want to work towards that finish. They are a proposed approach, not evidence that the next marathon will repeat the half-marathon result.

Sources and calculation method

This account combines my recollection with my Strava activity summaries and kilometre split tables. Paces and heart rates are transcribed as displayed. Block averages are calculated from full-kilometre lap paces, retaining the decimal before display. Download the transcribed data and calculation notes.

The comparison does not establish which training, fueling or equipment change caused the improvement. No missing distance is filled in, no race-half split is inferred, and no activity badge is treated as a direct comparison between these races. Editorial policy · Corrections.
