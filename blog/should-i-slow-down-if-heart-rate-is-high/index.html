Pacing decisions

Should I slow down if my heart rate is high?

An unexpectedly high reading needs context. Check how you feel, what the run is asking of you and whether the measurement is plausible. Do not force a pace or ignore symptoms to satisfy a watch zone.

First check for symptoms

Stop and seek urgent help for chest pain, fainting or severe unusual breathlessness, especially with palpitations. Confusion or collapse during exercise in heat is also an emergency. A suspected sensor error is not a reason to continue through these symptoms. Healthdirect Australia: heart palpitations and Healthdirect Australia: heatstroke and heat illness describe signs requiring help.

Check the reading without chasing it

An abrupt jump unrelated to effort can reflect a measurement problem. Check the fit and placement recommended by your device manufacturer, and review the trace later. Do not repeatedly accelerate or slow down to manipulate an implausible number. If the elevation is sustained or recurrent and unexplained, seek advice.

Perceived effort also has limits. Feeling comfortable does not automatically establish that an unusual heart-rate pattern is harmless.

Decide what to do today

If an easy run feels substantially harder than intended, slow down, shorten it or stop. There is no obligation to preserve the planned duration. If the reading changes with a hill and settles afterward while you remain well, use the whole context rather than one spike.

During a race, compare your actual splits with your plan. If effort is too high for the remaining distance, revise the pace or goal. Taking extra fluid, salt or carbohydrate is not a diagnostic test for a high heart rate.

A race example with limits

In my two Run Melbourne half marathons, recorded average heart rate was 171 bpm in 2025 and 164 bpm in 2026. The faster 2026 finish included a rise to 174 bpm in kilometre 21. Those are my activity observations, not target numbers for another runner or proof of a specific training adaptation.

When to review the pattern

Record the route, conditions, duration, symptoms and sensor used. Repeated unusual readings or difficulty deserve a review with an appropriate health professional. For training interpretation, see cardiac drift and combining pace, heart rate and perceived effort.
