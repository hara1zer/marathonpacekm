Pacing decisions

Negative split marathon pacing: plan the second half realistically

A faster second half can be satisfying. The useful decision is how much later speed your opening plan demands, and whether your training and the course support it.

Three different things runners call a strong finish

A negative split means completing the second half faster than the first. Even pacing means similar speed across the race. Even effort allows speed to change with terrain and weather. A late acceleration can feel excellent without producing a negative split over the whole race.

In my 2025 and 2026 half-marathon comparison, my final two full recorded kilometres in 2026 were 4:53 and 4:51/km. Those laps support my recollection of finishing strongly; they do not, on their own, measure the two race halves.

Choose what you want the finish to achieve

For a runner rebuilding confidence after a late fade, finishing in control may matter more than squeezing out every possible second. For a time goal, holding back also has an arithmetic cost: the remaining distance must fit into less time. Decide which outcome matters before choosing the size of a negative split.

When to reconsider the plan

  • Your intended second-half pace is not supported by recent training.
  • The second half is hillier, more exposed or likely to be hotter.
  • You are already struggling with effort or fuel at halfway.
  • Maintaining the target would require a large catch-up rather than a gradual change.

There is no weekly-mileage cutoff on this page that qualifies a runner for negative splitting. A first marathon, a slower finish target or lower mileage does not automatically rule it out. Those details inform goal selection; they do not establish a universal pacing rule.

Do not confuse a deliberate start with a recovery plan

A controlled opening is planned before the race. A catch-up plan is a response to time already lost. If congestion, a stop or difficult conditions change the schedule, first reassess the finish target. Avoid treating the second-half column as a debt that must be repaid.

The time-in-the-bank calculator shows how later slowing can offset an early gain. The arithmetic also works in reverse: allowing extra time early increases the speed required later.

Use checkpoints with effort

Choose a realistic pace reference, settle without surging, and reassess at halfway and around 30 km. If you still feel controlled, a gradual increase remains an option. If effort is deteriorating, revise the goal instead of forcing an acceleration. See mid-race decisions and the quick split-strategy comparison.

What to practise before committing

Use sessions that already fit your training load to practise pace changes, fuel and effort checks. Record how the later running feels and how you recover. You do not need a new exhausting simulation simply to earn permission to use a pacing strategy. The long-run guide compares possible structures.

A strong finish is useful evidence

My stronger 2026 half marathon changed what I wanted from a finish. My later Sydney Marathon experience showed that confidence over 21.1 km still needed support over 42.195 km. Use that distinction when setting a marathon target with the predictor.

Questions about negative splits

Can a beginner run a negative split?

Yes. Experience alone does not determine the split. Start with an achievable overall target and account for the course, conditions and later pace required.

Does finishing fast prove the opening was perfect?

No. A fast finish can reflect good control, a favourable final section or unused capacity. It does not identify one optimal earlier pace.

Should I keep trying to negative split if the race becomes harder?

Reassess effort and the finish target. A planned acceleration is optional; it should not override deteriorating conditions or concerning symptoms.
