Marathon hill pacing: climbs, descents and effort

Build a plan around where the climbs and descents occur. Total ascent alone cannot tell you the pace to hold, or whether lost time can be recovered.

By Davin Pinto · Updated 23 October 2026 · Editorial policy · Corrections

Read the profile before adjusting the target

Mark the start and end of major climbs, the descent after each one, and the next level section where you can reassess. Note footing, narrow sections and water access separately. Two courses with the same total ascent can distribute it very differently.

For example, 100 metres of rise spread over 5 km is an average 2% grade; the same rise over 1 km is 10%. Both add 100 metres of ascent. They do not impose the same running task, and neither number supplies a universal seconds-per-kilometre adjustment.

Why the same pace costs more uphill

Minetti and colleagues' treadmill study measured energy cost across gradients. Cost changed with slope; downhill was not simply the reverse of uphill. This supports allowing pace to vary with terrain. It does not validate a personal marathon-time penalty from an elevation-gain total.

Let pace slow if holding it creates more effort than planned. Compare breathing and perceived effort with your intended race effort, allowing for heart rate's delayed response. Avoid forcing a new stride or cadence simply to satisfy a generic hill rule.

A descent can feel easier while adding muscular load

Downhill running involves eccentric muscle actions. The downhill-running review by Bontemps and colleagues discusses muscle damage, performance effects and adaptation. A lower oxygen cost at some slopes does not mean a descent is recovery without a cost.

Keep speed within the limits of the surface, visibility and crowd. Avoid reaching forward to chase seconds, but do not interpret “avoid braking” as permission to lose control. Quad discomfort alone cannot diagnose muscle damage or predict how much you will slow later.

Include the climb in the finish-time arithmetic

Suppose a hypothetical four-hour runner reaches 30 km in 2:53:00 after a hilly section. The remaining 12.195 km must take 1:07:00 to retain four hours: 5:29.6/km. That is faster than the original average of 5:41.3/km.

Continuing at the original average from 30 km instead gives approximately 4:02:22. The climb's time cost does not create an obligation to run the next descent faster. Compare the revised target's checkpoints, the remaining profile and your effort before choosing.

This arithmetic assumes no additional stops and the stated pace over all remaining distance. It is not a model of fatigue or proof that either option is achievable.

Use practical checkpoints on rolling terrain

At 10 km, check whether you are already forcing climbs to defend average pace. At halfway, inspect how you are controlling descents and whether later hills change the plan. Around 30 km, reassess the target with the actual distance remaining; do not schedule a compulsory late push.

Keep fueling tied to your practised timing and accessible water. A crest is not universally the best place to eat. The 2026 Sydney course archive offers a route-specific example, while the personal Sydney review records one runner's experience.

Prepare within the workload you already tolerate

Introduce relevant terrain gradually. Record the slope, duration, effort and later soreness so that a repeat is a comparable session. Avoid increasing long-run duration and downhill intensity together merely because a race is hilly.

A familiar controlled descent can teach you about your footing and effort; it does not reproduce a marathon's final kilometres. If you have pain or are returning from injury, seek individual guidance before adding that load.

Three assumptions to check

  • A net-downhill route still contains climbs and can add substantial muscular loading.
  • A grade-adjusted pace is a model output, not a direct measurement of your effort or a promise of sustainable race pace.
  • The conditions calculator's flat, rolling and hilly percentages are illustrative settings, not a calibrated course model.

Use the conditions scenarios to explore an alternative target, then inspect the assumptions. Keep the organiser's current route and safety advice alongside the plan.

Hill-pacing questions

Should I hold the same kilometre pace on every climb?

Only if the effort remains appropriate. A slower uphill split can be consistent with the intended effort; the route determines where a pace comparison is meaningful.

Can I make up all the lost time downhill?

There is no guarantee. Work out the later pace requirement, then consider effort, surface and muscular load before deciding whether to revise the finish target.

Does total ascent tell me how many minutes to add?

No. Gradient, placement, descent, footing, weather and the runner's preparation matter. Use a course profile and actual observations rather than one universal conversion.

Sources and limits

  • Minetti et al. (2002), energy cost across uphill and downhill slopes.
  • Bontemps et al. (2020), downhill running review.
  • Lemire et al. (2021), running economy across slopes.
