Pacing decisions

Marathon pacing in headwinds and tailwinds

Wind changes the effort required for a pace, and the effect varies across the route. Map the exposed sections and choose a controlled effort before deciding which split to chase.

By Davin Pinto · Updated 1 October 2026 · Editorial policy · Corrections

Why opposite winds do not simply cancel

Relative air speed changes when wind aligns with or opposes your movement. Aerodynamic drag increases approximately with the square of relative air speed under a simplified steady-flow model. That describes drag, not the whole metabolic cost of running.

For example, running at 12 km/h with a steady 6 km/h headwind gives 18 km/h relative air speed; the same tailwind gives 6 km/h. Compared with still-air relative speed of 12 km/h, squared-speed ratios are 2.25 and 0.25. You cannot convert those ratios directly into a 125% effort increase or a finish-time adjustment.

Read exposure as well as the forecast number

A forecast wind speed does not describe every street. Buildings, trees, turns, gusts and the direction of travel change exposure. Check the route and note sustained open sections. Forecast speed alone cannot tell you how many seconds per kilometre to concede.

Let pace respond to the conditions

If maintaining target pace into a headwind pushes effort beyond what you intended, ease. Do not deliberately increase effort by a fixed RPE amount because a generic rule says it will save time.

A tailwind may allow faster running at a similar effort, but it does not create a guaranteed opportunity to recover every second. Avoid converting every sheltered section into an acceleration.

Mark three useful places on the course

  • An exposed stretch where you expect pace to vary.
  • A sheltered section where you can reassess effort without immediately chasing time.
  • A later checkpoint where you will reconsider the overall target.

If 5 km takes 30 seconds longer than planned, that is six seconds per kilometre over that section. Whether trying to recover it is sensible depends on the remaining course and your condition. Use the remaining-distance calculation before treating lost time as a debt.

Use shelter without chasing a faster group

A suitable group can reduce wind exposure, but joining it is useful only if its speed fits your plan. Leave space, keep your line predictable and respect other runners. Avoid sprinting between packs or crowding someone’s heels. Laboratory findings about drafting at particular speeds do not establish one universal marathon saving.

Prioritise control in gusts

Keep your footing and direction predictable. Avoid abrupt lane changes or forcing an unfamiliar running technique. In strong crosswinds, safety and course instructions matter more than maintaining cadence or a particular split.

Practise decisions in ordinary sessions

On an appropriate familiar route, notice how effort changes when direction changes. Keep the session’s intended effort instead of forcing identical splits. This can improve your understanding of that route, but does not create a personal wind-conversion formula from one run.

Common interpretation errors

  • Assuming a forecast applies equally to every course section.
  • Treating a tailwind as permission to sprint.
  • Mistaking GPS error for a direct effect of wind; obstruction and signal quality need their own check.
  • Assuming another runner’s comfortable-looking pace must be appropriate for you.

Questions about windy races

Should I change my finish goal?

Reconsider it if sustained exposure makes the original effort unrealistic. The whole course and your response matter more than a single wind-speed band.

Should I push harder into the headwind?

This guide does not prescribe that. For a recreational marathon plan, avoid forcing the target when it creates unexpectedly high effort.

Will a tailwind cancel the headwind?

Not necessarily. Aerodynamic effects, route exposure and the effort you choose make the balance more complicated.
