My fueling lessons

My marathon fueling experiments: what I learned from homemade and commercial gels

By Davin Pinto · Published 2 October 2026 · Updated 29 October 2026 · Personal experience, not a product ranking

The Running Lab / Personal fueling experiment

First published 2 October 2026 · Revised 29 October 2026

Read the fueling experience and the arithmetic separately

Question
What can I keep consuming while running, rather than merely planning to carry?
Available evidence
My homemade-mix and commercial-gel recollections, the 9 August rehearsal context, and selected recorded laps. The four-hour, 25 g example is a separate calculation.
Reading boundary
This is not a controlled product comparison or a verified recipe. Exact commercial variants, complete carbohydrate and fluid intake, and the cause of symptoms remain unknown.

Before Sydney Marathon, I wanted a practical way to carry enough carbohydrate for a long race. I tried making my own gels with maltodextrin and fructose, and I used commercial gels in training. The homemade mix looked promising as a way to control the serving, but taking it while running was a different problem.

It made my mouth much drier. Later, at Sydney, even a familiar commercial gel became unappealing when the race got difficult. Those experiences changed the question from “How many gels should I carry?” to “What can I actually keep consuming after several hours?”

This is a record of my experience, not a controlled comparison of brands or a tested homemade recipe. I do not have a complete record of my Sydney carbohydrate and fluid intake. The arithmetic example below uses an explicitly hypothetical serving, not the carbohydrate content of the products I used.

Making the serving was not the same as making it easy to take

The attraction of homemade gels was flexibility: I could experiment with the amount of carbohydrate and use reusable pouches, rather than treating every packet as the same-sized serving. I was also interested in carrying fewer individual gels.

My experiments centred on maltodextrin and fructose. I considered different serving sizes and consistencies, but an intended formulation is not proof of what ended up in every pouch. I am not publishing a gram-by-gram recipe because I do not have a verified, repeatable version to recommend.

The clearest result was how the mix felt. It was thick and made my mouth much drier. The more fluid, PURE-style consistency was closer to what I wanted than that thick homemade texture. That is a preference about taking the gel, not evidence that one formulation delivers carbohydrate better or prevents stomach problems.

I also do not describe the homemade mix as a validated hydrogel. Trying ingredients or aiming for a texture does not establish that a home preparation behaves like a commercial product.

The 9 August long run did not isolate one cause

On my 32 km long run on 9 August 2026, I went out too hard. My recorded early two-kilometre laps were around 5:16–5:31/km. During the run I was using a thick homemade carbohydrate mix, experienced stomach cramping around 21 km and a stitch around 25 km, and stopped for water and carbohydrate. I also needed commercial gels.

The final two full recorded laps eased to approximately 6:28 and 6:47/km. The long-run comparison in my Sydney review shows the early range and late laps.

It would be convenient to say that the homemade gel caused the whole problem. I cannot establish that from this run. The early effort and the difficulty taking the mix happened in the same session, and I did not hold the other variables constant.

What I can say is that this version was not easy for me to use during that rehearsal. I want future tests to record the formulation, effort, drinks and timing together. Otherwise, a bad run leaves me with a memory of discomfort but little evidence about what to change.

A familiar gel still became difficult late at Sydney

In training I had used Winners and PURE gels on roughly a one-every-20-minutes schedule. At Sydney I used Winners, with the same intended frequency. I have not recorded the exact variant consistently enough to assign a carbohydrate amount to that statement.

The race started feeling difficult a little before 30 km, and I first walked around 31–32 km. Around that change, my stomach no longer felt like taking more gels. I felt empty, but another gel was becoming unappealing. Earlier I had a mild stitch that I could manage with controlled breathing; this later feeling was different.

That account explains a problem with executing the plan. It does not show exactly how many grams I consumed, what I absorbed or whether a carbohydrate shortfall was the main cause of the slowdown. “I intended one every 20 minutes” and “I consumed the full schedule” are different claims.

I do not want to turn this into a verdict that Winners is unsuitable or that PURE would have fixed Sydney. The exact variants, conditions and late-run experience matter. My useful conclusion is narrower: I need to rehearse the complete plan and keep a record of what I actually take.

Every 20 minutes describes timing, not the full-race intake

A label supplies the carbohydrate per serving. The schedule supplies the number of servings. Both are needed before I can compare the plan with an hourly target.

Consider a hypothetical four-hour run with 25 g of carbohydrate per gel, no carbohydrate from drinks, and the first gel at 20 minutes. Continuing every 20 minutes before the finish gives 11 gels: at 20, 40, 60 minutes and so on through 220 minutes.

The fueling calculator makes that distinction between a selected schedule and a target. The finish-time fueling guide has further worked examples. Neither can establish my actual race intake from a recollection of the intended interval.

What I want to test and record next time

For a separate pre-race food record, the carb-loading worksheet keeps planned and recorded portions distinct. It does not turn these on-run experiments into a tested loading protocol.

I want the next useful test to be something I can repeat, rather than changing the mix, pace and drink plan together and then guessing which change mattered. It does not need to begin with another very long or hard run.

These are the notes I intend to keep. They are not results of tests I have already completed:

  • Exact product or mix: variant, label carbohydrate and serving size; for a homemade mix, weighed ingredients and final portions.
  • Planned versus actual intake: when I intended to take it, when I did, and whether I finished the serving.
  • Drinks: what I consumed, with measured amounts where available and uncertainty where cups cannot be measured reliably.
  • Run context: duration, effort, conditions and any stops.
  • Experience over time: mouth feel, willingness to eat, stomach symptoms and when they appeared.

Burke and colleagues' review of distance-running nutrition emphasises an individually rehearsed nutrition plan that weighs carbohydrate and fluid needs against practical access and gut discomfort. That is useful context for my next tests, not proof of what caused my symptoms.

I am still interested in homemade gels, but a serving that is attractive on paper is not a finished race-day solution. I also need to check commercial products by their exact labels rather than assuming that a brand name means one carbohydrate dose.

The aim is a plan I can keep using when tired. Alongside my proposed post-Sydney rebuild, that means practising controlled effort and fueling together, then judging the later part of the run as carefully as the start.

Sources and what remains unknown

This account combines my August gel-trial recollections, recorded laps from the 9 August long run, my personal Sydney review dated 5 September 2026 and subsequent race recollections. The 25 g serving and four-hour schedule are illustrative calculations only.

The exact homemade recipe, commercial variants, complete race intake and contribution of each possible cause to the slowdown remain unresolved. This is a personal account, not individual sports-nutrition advice or a recommendation to reproduce the mix.
