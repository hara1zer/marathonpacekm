Race execution

Marathon fueling by finish time: schedules that add up

Use your expected duration to count the carbohydrate in a complete race plan. The examples below show every gel, the contribution from drinks and the average actually supplied. Choose an intake you have rehearsed before using the arithmetic.

Published by Davin Pinto · Reviewed 2 October 2026 · Editorial policy · Corrections

Build the plan from products and time

Three things belong on the same page: expected race duration, carbohydrate per serving, and the times you intend to consume it. A list of five gels is not an hourly target until you divide their grams by the full race duration. A gel carried as a spare contributes nothing to that total unless you consume it.

Keep the plan separate from predictions about why someone slowed down. Pacing, conditions, preparation and stomach tolerance can all affect execution. My Sydney experience records difficulty taking more gels late in the race; it does not establish a single cause.

Choose a practiced intake; finish time sets the total

Jeukendrup’s carbohydrate review discusses approximately 30–60 g/h for prolonged exercise and higher intakes, up to about 90 g/h, for longer endurance events using suitable carbohydrate combinations. These are context-dependent sports-nutrition ranges, not a rule that a particular marathon pace requires a particular dose.

A five-hour runner does not automatically need a lower hourly intake than a three-hour runner. Duration changes the amount to carry. Tolerance, preparation, product choice and individual needs determine a workable hourly plan. The 50, 60 and 75 g/h examples here demonstrate arithmetic; they are not assigned to runners by speed.

The two calculations to check

Planned total = practiced target (g/h) × duration (hours)
Actual planned average = (gel grams + drink grams + other on-course carbohydrate) ÷ duration

For a four-hour race, five 25 g gels supply 125 g, or 31.25 g/h. Seven supply 175 g, or 43.75 g/h. Neither supplies 60 g/h without another carbohydrate source. Eight 25 g gels plus 40 g from drinks supply 240 g, or 60 g/h.

Use the product label’s carbohydrate value, not packet weight. Pre-start food or a pre-start gel is recorded separately here. All scheduled race gels below are strictly before the finish; an extra backup is not counted.

Examples finishing before 2:45

2:30 race: 60 g/h arithmetic

5 gels of 30 g each, at elapsed times 0:15:00, 0:45:00, 1:15:00, 1:45:00, 2:15:00. This supplies 150 g from gels, with no drink carbohydrate counted: 150 g ÷ 2.5 hours = 60 g/h.

The final planned gel is at 2:15, fifteen minutes before the hypothetical finish. The label is a duration category for finding the example, not a reason to choose 60 g/h.

Examples finishing around 3:00–3:30

3:00 race: 75 g/h arithmetic

9 gels of 25 g each, at elapsed times 0:10:00, 0:30:00, 0:50:00, 1:10:00, 1:30:00, 1:50:00, 2:10:00, 2:30:00, 2:50:00. This supplies 225 g from gels, with no drink carbohydrate counted: 225 g ÷ 3 hours = 75 g/h.

3:30 race: 60 g/h arithmetic

7 gels of 30 g each, at elapsed times 0:15:00, 0:45:00, 1:15:00, 1:45:00, 2:15:00, 2:45:00, 3:15:00. This supplies 210 g from gels, with no drink carbohydrate counted: 210 g ÷ 3.5 hours = 60 g/h.

The two examples deliberately use different serving sizes and intervals. “A gel every twenty minutes” supplies different amounts when a packet contains 20, 25 or 30 g.

Examples finishing around 4:00–4:30

4:00 race: 60 g/h arithmetic

8 gels of 25 g each, at elapsed times 0:15:00, 0:45:00, 1:15:00, 1:45:00, 2:15:00, 2:45:00, 3:15:00, 3:45:00. This supplies 200 g from gels plus 40 g from drinks: 240 g ÷ 4 hours = 60 g/h.

4:30 race: 60 g/h arithmetic

9 gels of 25 g each, at elapsed times 0:15:00, 0:45:00, 1:15:00, 1:45:00, 2:15:00, 2:45:00, 3:15:00, 3:45:00, 4:15:00. This supplies 225 g from gels plus 45 g from drinks: 270 g ÷ 4.5 hours = 60 g/h.

The drink contribution in both examples averages 10 g/h. For example, a verified 6 g carbohydrate per 100 mL would require about 667 mL across four hours to provide 40 g. This is a conversion example, not a hydration target. Check the actual mix and the volume you can consume; a few unmeasured sips may provide much less.

Example finishing around 5:00

5:00 race: 50 g/h arithmetic

10 gels of 25 g each, at elapsed times 0:15:00, 0:45:00, 1:15:00, 1:45:00, 2:15:00, 2:45:00, 3:15:00, 3:45:00, 4:15:00, 4:45:00. This supplies 250 g from gels, with no drink carbohydrate counted: 250 g ÷ 5 hours = 50 g/h.

At five hours, the same ten 25 g gels average 50 g/h. If a practiced 60 g/h target were chosen instead, the planned total would be 300 g, leaving a 50 g gap to account for. Plan that gap before race day rather than quietly calling the existing schedule 60 g/h.

Match elapsed-time reminders to course access

Take the elapsed schedule to the race’s station map. At a four-hour even pace, a 1:15 reminder occurs near 13.19 km and a 1:45 reminder near 18.46 km. Those are arithmetic locations, not guaranteed water stations. Decide whether to shift a reminder to nearby water after checking the product instructions and rehearsing the timing.

The pace-band tool prints elapsed pacing checkpoints. The fueling calculator produces a separate fuel schedule; gel reminders are not printed inside the pace band. Keep both references available if you need both.

Count drinks without turning carb math into a fluid prescription

The ACSM fluid-replacement position stand emphasises individual variation in fluid needs. Temperature, sweating, access and tolerance matter. Do not force extra drink solely to close a carbohydrate gap; you can change the carbohydrate sources in a rehearsed plan instead.

Record carbohydrate and fluid separately. “Sports drink available” does not establish its concentration or the amount in a cup. Sodium needs also vary; taking electrolytes does not guarantee that cramps will be prevented. Use the event’s current product information and your experience rather than assuming every station is interchangeable.

Rehearse and record what you actually consume

  1. Use the exact packet sizes and drink mix you intend to race with.
  2. Record consumption times, missed servings and stomach symptoms during an appropriate long run.
  3. Calculate the supplied average from what you consumed, then compare it with the planned average.
  4. Change one part at a time: serving size, spacing, product or drink contribution. Do not make an untried increase on race morning.

A useful training record can say “six of eight gels consumed, 150 g, drink amount unknown”. It should not claim the intended 240 g was delivered when it was not. For training structure, see threshold work within a marathon block; the training label itself does not validate a fueling plan.

Find the gap before race day

  • Counting packets instead of grams: nine 20 g packets and nine 30 g packets differ by 90 g.
  • Counting optional fuel: show a base plan and a backup separately.
  • Ignoring duration: 200 g supplies 50 g/h over four hours, but 40 g/h over five.
  • Assuming cup contents: a drink brand alone does not establish carbohydrate consumed.
  • Using the last-minute serving to fix a shortfall: a gel at the finish is outside the on-course schedule here.

Write a plan you can audit

Before the race, write down expected duration, practiced target, grams per serving, first reminder, interval, drink contribution and a backup. Check that every listed serving fits before the finish and that the total divided by duration gives the stated average. Afterward, record what actually happened.

The fueling calculator can generate the timestamps. For a course-specific example, use Melbourne’s published 2026 station table.

Fueling arithmetic FAQ

How many gels do I need for a four-hour marathon?

There is no universal count. In this article’s 60 g/h example, eight 25 g gels plus 40 g from drinks supply 240 g over four hours. Eight gels without those drinks supply only 50 g/h.

Does a slower finish mean a lower hourly target?

No. Duration determines the total amount required for an already chosen hourly target. Tolerance, preparation and individual needs determine a workable intake.

Do backup gels count in the planned average?

Only if the base schedule includes consuming them. A carried spare is listed separately here.

Will the pace band print my gel schedule?

No. The pace band prints pacing checkpoints. The fueling calculator provides a separate elapsed-time fuel schedule.
