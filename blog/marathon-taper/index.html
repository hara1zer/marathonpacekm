Marathon taper: compare two- and three-week mileage

By Davin Pinto · Published 25 October 2026 · Research and worked examples

Your marathon taper should reduce the work you carry into race day. The awkward part is deciding what “reduce mileage” means when one calendar week also contains a 42.195 km race.

Start by keeping pre-race training and the marathon in separate totals. Then compare two- and three-week approaches against the running you have actually sustained. The worksheet below makes the arithmetic visible and lets you change every percentage.

How long should a marathon taper be?

Two and three weeks are both worth comparing. Your existing programme, accumulated fatigue and previous response to tapering provide context. Published research supports reducing training load before competition, but it does not identify one ideal schedule for every recreational marathoner.

Bosquet and colleagues' meta-analysis of competitive athletes found favourable results for a two-week taper with a substantial volume reduction while retaining intensity and frequency. It combined different sporting contexts; its percentages should not be read as a precise marathon race-week prescription.

In a different type of study, Smyth and Lawlor analysed more than 158,000 recreational marathoners. Longer, more disciplined tapers up to three weeks were associated with better performance than shorter or less disciplined alternatives. This was an observational analysis. It cannot promise the same time saving for someone who changes their taper.

A practical comparison asks: how much training comes out, which familiar sessions remain, and whether the schedule actually lets fatigue fall. Adding a last-minute test workout can defeat the point of the reduction.

Marathon taper mileage worksheet

Use a representative recent training week you have sustained and recovered from. An isolated peak or an aspirational mileage target can inflate every number. The defaults—80%, 60% and 40% remaining—are editable illustrations, not a research-derived personalised prescription.

Race week: 24.0 km before the race + 42.195 km in the marathon = 66.2 km including race day. Warm-ups and cool-downs belong in the pre-race total.

These are distance budgets. Choose individual sessions with your existing programme; percentages do not assess fatigue or injury.

“Forty percent remaining” and “cut forty percent” are different

For a 60 km reference week, 40% remaining means 24 km. A 40% reduction leaves 36 km. Those phrases differ by twelve kilometres, so write down whether a percentage describes the distance kept or the distance removed.

Training budget = reference distance × fraction remaining
60 km × 0.40 = 24 km before the race.

The three-week illustration therefore reads 48 km, then 36 km, then 24 km before the marathon. The two-week illustration reads 36 km, then 24 km before the marathon. Both use the same reference, not a percentage of the previous week's already reduced total.

These examples only demonstrate a declining sequence. They do not establish that 24 km is suitable for you. If you have been unwell, injured or unable to sustain the reference week, revisit the underlying plan rather than mechanically multiplying it.

The calendar-week total exceeds the 60 km reference because it includes the race. That does not mean the pre-race taper increased training. If you add race-morning jogging, record it separately too.

A runner accustomed to three days should not add days to copy this table. Someone who prefers to rest on Saturday should not feel obliged to run because a template contains a shakeout. Keep the pattern appropriate to the runner and the existing plan.

Keep the purpose of a workout while shortening its load

Maintaining some familiar intensity does not mean retaining an entire hard workout. Compare the whole session: pace minutes, recovery running, total duration and the days around it. A small amount of familiar pace work and a full race simulation are different demands.

Use the marathon-pace workout guide to count all the parts of a session. The long-run comparison also helps identify when a supposedly easy outing includes a substantial faster finish. Do not add distance just to fill a worksheet budget.

Gym work belongs in the same review. An experienced lifter may retain a small amount of familiar work, but introducing new lifts or chasing soreness in the final weeks adds an avoidable uncertainty. The strength guide's race-approach section explains how to reduce sets and preserve recovery.

Keep practised fueling arrangements visible as training falls. Use the race fueling arithmetic to check your on-course schedule; this taper worksheet does not prescribe carbohydrate loading or fluid intake.

When the final weeks do not go to plan

A missed run does not create a debt to repay before race day. Review the reason it was missed, the training already completed and whether the race goal still fits. The disrupted-plan guide covers that decision in more detail.

New focal pain, worsening symptoms, illness or pain that changes how you run deserves assessment. Calling it “taper madness” cannot establish that it is harmless. A percentage calculator cannot clear you to race.

For ordinary uncertainty about pace, compare representative training and race evidence in the goal-selection guide. Avoid using a late all-out session as a confidence test. Save the pace band, confirm logistics and leave space for sleep and travel.

Marathon taper questions

Should the marathon count in taper-week mileage?

Show it separately when comparing pre-race training. You can also record the whole calendar-week total, but label both numbers so the race does not distort the comparison.

Does tapering mean stopping running completely?

Published taper research generally concerns reduced training load, often with familiar frequency and intensity retained. It does not mean everyone should stop running, nor that someone with injury symptoms should keep training.

Will a three-week taper make me lose fitness?

The recreational-marathon study found favourable associations for disciplined tapers up to three weeks. It does not determine your individual response. Judge the actual reduction and session content rather than the number of weeks alone.

Sources and worksheet assumptions

  • Bosquet L and colleagues (2007), Effects of tapering on performance: a meta-analysis. Competitive-athlete evidence across sporting contexts.
  • Smyth B and Lawlor A (2021), Longer Disciplined Tapers Improve Marathon Performance for Recreational Runners. Observational marathon analysis; association does not establish an individual treatment effect.
  • Worksheet calculation method. All weeks use one reference distance. Race distance is added separately; changing units reinterprets the entered reference distance in the selected unit.

The daily schedule and default percentages were written to illustrate accounting. They were not tested as a standalone training programme.
