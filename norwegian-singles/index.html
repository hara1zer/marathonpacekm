Norwegian Singles: what the term means and what the evidence supports

A guide to the terminology, its connection to lactate-guided elite training, and the limits of borrowing it for a recreational running week.

What “singles” means on this site

We use “Norwegian Singles” for a recreational adaptation that puts one controlled threshold session on a quality day, rather than a morning-and-afternoon double. The term is used informally and schedules vary. Single does not mean exactly one threshold workout per week. One weekly session is a cautious example used in our plans, not the definition of the method.

The practical question is what happens to your whole week when you add the session: easy-run effort, long-run execution and recovery all matter.

Separate research, first-person history and adaptation

Casado and colleagues’ 2023 paper examines lactate-guided intervals within a high-volume, mostly low-intensity approach. The 2024 Norwegian session-model paper describes practice gathered from world-class coaches. These are useful sources for understanding the elite context; neither establishes a tested dose for recreational “singles”.

Bakken’s account is a first-person explanation of his own model, not a controlled trial. The recreational weeks on this site are our illustrations. Keeping those three categories separate avoids claiming that an example schedule has been scientifically validated.

Why broken work can be easier to review

Intervals provide repeated opportunities to check pace and effort. If the third repetition needs a sprint to preserve the first repetition’s pace, that is information to respond to, not a reason to finish faster. The breaks also change the session: 24 minutes broken into repetitions is not identical to 24 continuous minutes.

A cautious four-run illustration

For someone already accustomed to workouts: Tuesday has one quality session; Thursday and Saturday stay easy; Sunday is a comfortable long run. Monday, Wednesday and Friday are rest days. This is one possible layout, not a validated Norwegian protocol. A hard Sunday finish would add another quality demand.

The three-to-six-day plans show the actual calendar and what changes between versions.

A race result cannot locate your lactate threshold

The pace calculator applies stated offsets to race pace. It does not measure blood lactate or establish a physiological threshold. Use its slower output as a starting estimate only, and change pace or stop the work when the session is becoming a race. A device reading cannot override symptoms or poor recovery.

Keep marathon preparation visible

Threshold work answers a different question from handling several hours of running, fuel and changing conditions. The marathon adaptation page shows how a faster workout can displace long-run recovery if both are treated as priorities every week.

Three mistakes to catch in the training log

  • Recording only pace, without work minutes and recoveries.
  • Adding threshold sessions while leaving every existing hard session in place.
  • Calling a fixed lactate number or calculator output a universal threshold.

Start from the problem you are trying to solve

If you are still building a stable easy-running routine, the training label is not the missing ingredient. If your current quality day already leaves the long run compromised, adding another one is unlikely to solve that scheduling problem. The suitability checklist helps identify what to change first.

Terminology FAQ

Does singles mean once a week?

No. On this site it means one threshold session on a quality day. One per week is a cautious example, not the definition.

Is the recreational plan a proven Norwegian protocol?

No. The linked papers discuss elite practice and training concepts. This site’s sample calendars are illustrations rather than a validated individual prescription.
